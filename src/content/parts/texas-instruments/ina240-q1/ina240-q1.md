## How it works

The INA240-Q1 is a zero-drift current-sense amplifier that sits across a shunt resistor. It measures VSENSE = VIN+ − VIN−, multiplies it by a fixed gain of 20, 50, 100 or 200 V/V (the four versions), and adds a level set by the two reference pins. The datasheet never writes the output equation. Our reading, which agrees with all of its reference-pin figures (8-3 to 8-8), is:

OUT = G × VSENSE + (VREF1 + VREF2) / 2

**Common mode that doesn't depend on the supply.** The inputs accept −4 V to 80 V while the part runs from 2.7 V to 5.5 V. A 5 V supply can therefore watch a shunt in the output of a 40 V motor bridge (the datasheet's example), in a high-side switch, or in a ground return. The allowance below ground is there for the flyback period of a solenoid, when the current keeps flowing and the sensed node swings under ground. The output is limited to the supply, 0 V to VS, however large the common mode is.

**Enhanced PWM rejection.** In a PWM drive the common-mode voltage at the shunt jumps between ground and the bridge supply at every edge. The datasheet's explanation is a high AC CMRR (93 dB typical at 50 kHz) together with the 400 kHz bandwidth, which it says gives minimal output transients and ringing, compared with the usual approach of simply raising the bandwidth. It is a property of the input stage, with no pin or setting for it.

**Accuracy.** The zero-drift input gives ±25 µV maximum offset (±5 µV typical) with 250 nV/°C maximum drift. The gain error is ±0.20% maximum (±0.05% typical) with 2.5 ppm/°C maximum drift, and the DC CMRR is 132 dB typical, 120 dB minimum. The electrical table gives one set of these numbers for all four gains.

**Unidirectional or bidirectional.** The two reference pins set where the output sits with no input. Ground gives a unidirectional output that rises with current, the supply gives one that falls, and one pin on each rail gives mid-supply for current in both directions. An external reference gives a precise midpoint. See the table under Example application.

**Four gains.** A higher gain gives the same output swing for a smaller shunt voltage, so a smaller shunt resistor and less dissipation, which the 100 and 200 V/V versions allow while keeping the offset below 25 µV. The versions differ in gain and in two other numbers: the reference voltage rejection ratio and the settling time the datasheet gives.

## Example application

The datasheet gives values only for the shunts and the supplies. It names no part for any other component and no designators, so the parts tables say "Not given", and it works no numbers beyond the shunt choice, so the worked examples below are calculated here. Two points from the text. The two reference pins are interchangeable. And they are meant to go to a supply, to ground or to a low-impedance reference: with a divider, the datasheet advises against using OUT as a single-ended signal because the internal impedance shifts hurt the specifications, and says to take OUT against the divider voltage with a differential ADC instead. The block diagram shows a 50 kΩ resistor inside on each reference pin. The supply-referenced connection is the one the datasheet suggests for circuits where the output must be up and stable before power is applied to the load.

The reference pins also add error. The "reference voltage rejection ratio" adds offset when the reference isn't at VS / 2. Table 8-2 gives the term as RVRR × |VS / 2 − VREF|, and the design table (calculated) below puts numbers on it: with a ground reference on a 5 V supply the INA240A1-Q1 adds 50 µV typical, which is twice its 25 µV maximum offset, where the A2 and A4 add 5 µV.

### Choosing RSENSE

The datasheet's method (Table 8-1, first design table) is: choose the full-scale current and the output voltage you want at it, divide the voltage by the gain to get the shunt voltage, divide that by the current to get RSENSE, and check the power. For 10 A and 3 V it gives 15 mΩ and 1.5 W with the 20 V/V version and 1.5 mΩ and 0.15 W with the 200 V/V version. We rechecked that arithmetic and it holds. The text adds the trade-off: a larger shunt voltage lowers the offset's share of the reading and raises the power and the heating, which changes the shunt itself.

The part has no limit on the shunt voltage other than what the output can do (the differential input is rated to ±80 V), so the output window sets the range. We take the window the gain error is specified in, GND + 50 mV to VS − 200 mV. At 5 V with a mid-supply reference that gives ±2.3 V at the output (2.3 V up to VS − 0.2 V, 2.45 V down to GND + 0.05 V) and so a full-scale shunt voltage of 2.3 V ÷ gain: 115, 46, 23 and 11.5 mV for the four versions. The second design table turns that into full-scale currents by shunt value, and the third does it for 3.3 V. With a ground reference (current in one direction) the window is 4.75 V wide instead, which the fourth table gives.

At the low end the offset matters. The fifth table converts the offset to a current: 5 µV typical or 25 µV maximum across 10 mΩ is 0.5 mA or 2.5 mA, and across 1 mΩ it is 5 mA or 25 mA. A larger shunt helps in proportion, at the price of power (the shunt power column, at 1 A).

### Total error

The datasheet describes the total error as the root-sum-square of the offset error, the gain error and the nonlinearity, where the offset has three parts: the initial offset, an addition from the common-mode voltage away from 12 V (divided by the CMRR), and an addition from a reference away from VS / 2 (the RVRR term). We recomputed both of its worked examples (Tables 8-2 and 8-3, INA240A4-Q1 at VSENSE = 10 mV) and got its results: 0.07% at 12 V common mode with a mid-supply reference, and 0.15% at 60 V with a ground reference (the offset terms add up to 14 µV there: 5 µV initial, 12 µV from common mode, 5 µV from the reference). The seventh design table (calculated) applies the same method across shunt voltages at the conditions of the first example, and the sixth and the one on common mode give the added terms. Two caveats of ours. The datasheet's examples use the typical CMRR (132 dB), while the specified minimum is 120 dB, which is four times worse: over the full −4 V to 80 V range the minimum allows up to 68 µV from common mode at 80 V, more than the 25 µV offset limit. And the method has no temperature term: the offset drift (250 nV/°C maximum) and the gain drift (2.5 ppm/°C maximum) come on top of it away from 25 °C.

### Input filtering

The datasheet says input filters aren't required and aren't recommended, and gives the rules for the case where one is wanted anyway. Keep the series resistors to 10 Ω or less, because the internal bias network draws different currents from the two inputs when there is a differential voltage (Figure 9-2), and that difference across the resistors becomes an error in the shunt voltage. Equation 2 gives the gain error factor as 3000 ÷ (RS + 3000), and the gain error as 100 − 100 × factor. The last design table reproduces Table 9-1 (5 Ω: 0.17%, 10 Ω: 0.33%, 100 Ω: 3.23%) and adds the common values in between. Resistor mismatch makes it worse, the text says. We read RS as the resistance in each input, since Figure 9-1 draws one in each and the equation has a single value. The alternative the text offers is a filter at the output, which makes the output impedance higher.

### Supply bypass

The text recommends a 0.1 µF capacitor as close as possible to the supply and ground pins, with more capacitance if the supply is noisy or high-impedance. The figures leave it out.

### Worked example: the solenoid circuit

Calculated here from the datasheet's values. The circuit is Figure 9-5 with INA240A4-Q1 (200 V/V), a 10 mΩ shunt, VS = 5 V, REF1 at ground and REF2 at VS.

- **Transfer.** OUT = 2.5 V + 200 × VSENSE, and VSENSE = I × 10 mΩ, so every amp moves the output by 2 V.
- **At the valve's 500 mA.** VSENSE is 5 mV, the output is 3.5 V, and the shunt dissipates 2.5 mW.
- **Range.** The output reaches VS − 0.2 V = 4.8 V at 1.15 A.
- **A choice the figure makes.** The solenoid current only flows one way, so half of the mid-supply output range is unused. With both reference pins at ground the range would be about 2.4 A (4.8 V ÷ 2 V per amp), but the output would clamp near ground below 25 mA (the 50 mV where the gain error specification starts, divided by 2 V per amp), which can matter when the valve is opening. The datasheet chose mid-supply and doesn't discuss the alternative.

## Layout

The datasheet's rule is the same for both packages: connect the shunt to the input pins with a Kelvin (four-wire) connection, so that only the resistance of the shunt itself appears between IN+ and IN–. A few extra milliohms of trace in the high-current path would be a significant fraction of a shunt that may be 1 to 10 mΩ, and it is a source of gain error that the datasheet's accuracy figures don't include. Figure 9-7 contrasts a Kelvin connection with a non-Kelvin one. Put the supply bypass capacitor close to VS and GND (0.1 µF recommended).

The two layout examples (Figure 11-1 for the TSSOP, Figure 11-2 for the SOIC) draw the shunt's two pads with a thin trace from each straight to IN– and IN+, a bypass capacitor between the VS and GND copper, NC tied to a ground via, and the reference pins wired for mid-supply: REF2 to VS and REF1 to ground in the TSSOP example, and REF1 to VS and REF2 to ground in the SOIC one, which are equivalent because the two pins are interchangeable.

The package drawings give land-pattern examples (the datasheet says IPC-7351 may have alternate designs). For the SOIC (D0008A, PDF page 33): eight pads 1.55 × 0.60 mm at 1.27 mm pitch, the two rows 5.4 mm apart (centre to centre as dimensioned), solder-mask details dimensioned at 0.07 mm, and a stencil example for a 0.125 mm sheet. For the TSSOP (PW0008A, PDF page 36): eight pads 1.5 × 0.45 mm at 0.65 mm pitch, rows 5.8 mm apart, solder-mask details dimensioned at 0.05 mm, also a 0.125 mm stencil. There is no exposed pad on either. A package page and KiCad footprint and symbol have not been made for this part.

Pin 1 is IN– on the SOIC (D), and the pins 1 to 8 are IN–, GND, REF2, NC, OUT, VS, REF1, IN+. On the TSSOP (PW) pin 1 is NC, and the pins 1 to 8 are NC, IN+, IN–, GND, VS, REF2, REF1, OUT.

## Things to watch

- **One datasheet, four gains.** The part number says which version it is, and the reference voltage rejection ratio (20 µV/V on the A1, 2 to 5 on the others) and the settling time (given for the A1 and A4 only) depend on it.
- **SOIC and TSSOP are not pin-compatible.** Pin 1 is IN– on one and NC on the other, and IN+ and OUT are on different numbers. A footprint made for one package can't be used for the other.
- **Grade 0 is SOIC only.** The TSSOP is Grade 1 (−40 to 125 °C). For the 150 °C parts you need an E-suffix SOIC.
- **The output is limited to the supply, so the range depends on VS and the reference.** At 5 V with a mid-supply reference the usable output is ±2.3 V around 2.5 V (calculated), and the gain error is only specified between GND + 50 mV and VS − 200 mV. At 3.3 V the swing is ±1.45 V.
- **The reference pins need a low-impedance drive.** The datasheet says to connect them to a supply, ground or a low-impedance reference, and not to use OUT single-ended behind a divider. The reference also changes the offset: 50 µV typical on the A1 with a ground reference at 5 V.
- **Common-mode offset at the minimum CMRR can exceed the offset limit.** 120 dB is 1 µV/V, so 68 µV at 80 V common mode (our arithmetic), against ±25 µV at 12 V. The datasheet's examples use the 132 dB typical figure.
- **The inputs draw a large bias current.** 90 µA typical at 12 V common mode, and the plot of bias current against common-mode voltage shows about 220 µA at 80 V (read by eye) and a slightly negative value below 0 V. That is why series resistors must stay at 10 Ω or less, and why a high-impedance source or a large filter changes the gain.
- **PWM rejection reduces the edge disturbance, it doesn't remove it.** Figure 9-4 shows narrow spikes of a few tenths of a volt at the switching edges (read by eye), and Figures 7-21 and 7-22 show a spike and a short recovery for a single common-mode step. Settling to 0.5% after a real change in current takes 9.6 to 9.8 µs, about one period of the 100 kHz PWM the datasheet mentions.
- **The output drive is not specified.** The swing figures are measured into 10 kΩ to ground. Figure 7-13 shows swing against current to 7 mA, and the maximum capacitive load is 1 nF with no sustained oscillation.
- **At 150 °C ambient the junction limit leaves no margin (our arithmetic).** The supply dissipates up to 5.5 V × 2.6 mA = 14.3 mW, which raises the junction by about 1.6 °C in the SOIC (RθJA 113.5 °C/W), so TJ would be about 151.6 °C at TA = 150 °C, above the 150 °C junction rating. The datasheet doesn't discuss it. At 125 °C the same arithmetic gives 126.6 °C (SOIC) or 127.1 °C (TSSOP), well inside the rating.
- **Start-up has a transient.** Figure 7-23 shows the output moving by itself for roughly 10 µs after the supply steps (read by eye), so don't read it right after power-up.
- **The shunt matters as much as the amplifier.** The datasheet gives no tolerance or temperature coefficient for the shunt, and a 1% shunt tolerance is five times the amplifier's 0.20% maximum gain error.
- **The circuits are examples.** The datasheet says the application sections are not part of TI's specification. The motor example's range of ±1.15 A and the solenoid's choice of reference are our calculations.

## Datasheet notes

- **Figure 9-5 says 12 V, the text and the curve say 24 V.** The figure prints 12 V on the supply, the text says the circuit measures a 24 V, 500 mA water valve, and the common-mode input in Figure 9-6 is about 24 V (read by eye). The page draws the figure as printed and says so.
- **Figure 9-3 says 40 V and the text says a 36 V motor.** Not a contradiction by itself (a bridge supply above the motor rating), but the text doesn't say so. Figure 9-4's common-mode swing is about 43 V by eye.
- **The NC pin is described three ways.** The pin table says "reserved" and to connect it to ground or leave it floating, the pin figures say "no internal connection", and the absolute maximum table lists NC among the inputs (GND − 0.3 V to VS + 0.3 V). The layout examples tie it to ground through a via. Revision E added the words about leaving it unconnected.
- **The output equation is never written.** The table row "reference divider accuracy" gives the output as |VREF1 − VREF2| / 2 with no input, which fits only a connection with one pin at ground (Figure 8-6). With both pins on one reference (Figure 8-5) the datasheet's own text says the output equals the reference. Our reading, the average of the two, fits every figure.
- **Equation numbering.** The text says the gain error factor is "shown in Equation 1", but it is Equation 2 (Equation 1 is the gain error). Table 9-1 agrees with Equation 2.
- **The error tables check out.** Tables 8-1, 8-2, 8-3 and 9-1 all recompute to the printed values.
- **Settling time is given for two gains of four.** The table lists the A1 and A4 only.
- **The addendum lists every part twice**, the second time with a .A suffix, with no explanation.
- **Revision history.** Revision E (December 2021) changed the SOIC body size from 4.00 × 3.91 mm to 4.90 × 3.91 mm in the device table, added "or left unconnected" for NC in the pin table, and changed the reference design link. Revision D (April 2020) added the functional-safety statement. Revision C (November 2018) went to production data and added the Grade 0 devices and the TSSOP production status. The tape-and-reel pages are dated July 2025 and the addendum November 2025, and the revision stays E.
- **Related parts.** The [SA59131](site:parts/silergy/sa59131/) from Silergy has the same -4 V to 80 V common-mode range and 2.7 V to 5.5 V supply, and its page lists the same pin numbering as this one in both its TSSOP and SOP packages, with the same reference-pin connections (compare the two pages; we have not compared the electrical specifications line by line).
