## How it works

The SA25901 is a synchronous buck regulator with both switches inside a SOT583 package. It switches at a constant frequency, 2.3 MHz, with peak current mode control, and has its compensation built in, so the circuit needs no external compensation network. The constant on-time and constant off-time parts elsewhere in this collection hold their frequency only approximately.

To soften EMI, the switching frequency is spread: it varies by ±8% around 2.3 MHz in a triangular pattern with a period of about 330 µs. In normal operation the frequency therefore sits between about 2.12 and 2.48 MHz. The electrical table lists 1.92–2.75 MHz as the range over the specification.

The MODE pin sets light-load behavior. Connected to ground, it enables power-save mode, with automatic transitions between PWM and PFM for better light-load efficiency. Pulled high (above 1.2 V), it forces PWM at every load. MODE must not be left floating. The datasheet's reference circuit ties MODE to VIN, so as drawn it runs in forced PWM (see Things to watch for what that costs at light load).

EN above 1.2 V enables the part, and EN below 0.4 V shuts it down and cuts the input current to about 2.5 µA (15 µA maximum). The output then rises in about 0.5 ms (0.2–0.8 ms). Input under-voltage lockout keeps switching off until VIN passes 2.7 V (2.6–2.8 V), with 100 mV of hysteresis. When the part is disabled, the SA25901BAT discharges the output through about 200 Ω.

The power-good pin (PG) is an open-drain output watching the feedback voltage with a window comparator. It goes high once FB reaches about 93% of the reference, and goes low if FB falls below about 90% or rises above about 110%, with a typical delay of 30 µs. It recovers from an over-voltage at about 107%. Pull it up to VIN or another supply through a resistor of 10 kΩ to 100 kΩ; the reference circuit uses 10 kΩ to VIN.

Current limiting is cycle by cycle. The top switch turns off at its peak limit (with a 1 µH inductor: 2.3 A minimum, 3.3 A typical, 4.3 A maximum), and the datasheet says the bottom-switch valley limit (0.85, 1.9 and 2.9 A) is enabled after two peak-limit cycles. The bottom switch also has a negative current limit (−1.5 A typical) on reverse current, which only flows in forced PWM. If the output stays below about 50% of its target for around 10 µs, the part enters hiccup mode: it switches off for 3 ms, then runs a new soft-start. Over-temperature protection shuts the part down at 165 °C typical and restarts it, after a soft-start, once it has cooled by about 20 °C.

When VIN falls toward the output voltage, the part can run at 100% duty, with the top switch held on, which is dropout. As the 60 ns minimum off-time is approached, the part lowers its switching frequency. The datasheet calls this adaptive frequency fold-back and says that, unlike traditional peak current control, it keeps the circuit stable in dropout.

## Example application

A 5 V to 1.2 V buck converter built from the circuit in Silergy's datasheet. At 2.3 MHz with spread-spectrum clocking the circuit needs only a 1.0 µH inductor and two small ceramic output capacitors; a MODE pin selects power-save or forced-PWM operation, and a power-good output reports the state of the rail. The datasheet covers the SA25901BAT and the SA25901ABAT, which differ only in output discharge. This section keeps the datasheet's component values, adds the reasoning behind each one, and works the numbers for 5 V in.

### Output voltage

The output is set by two resistors: VOUT = 0.6 V × (1 + RTOP ÷ RBOT). The datasheet recommends 1 kΩ to 1 MΩ for both. The reference circuit uses RTOP = RBOT = 51 kΩ, which gives exactly 1.200 V nominal. With the reference's ±1% tolerance (0.594–0.606 V) the output lands between 1.188 V and 1.212 V, before the resistors' own tolerance is added. For a 0.6 V output, the datasheet fits a link in place of RTOP and leaves RBOT out, tying FB to the output.

### Inductor

Choose the inductance for a ripple current of about 40% of the maximum load at the highest input voltage: L = VOUT × (1 − VOUT ÷ VIN,MAX) ÷ (fSW × ΔIL), with ΔIL = 0.4 × IOUT,MAX. For 1.2 V, 6 V in, 1 A and 2.3 MHz that gives 1.04 µH, and the datasheet uses 1.0 µH, for every output voltage in its table. The part tolerates variation in ripple, so the final value can differ from the calculation with little effect.

Two more checks apply. The saturation current must exceed the peak current, ISAT > IOUT,MAX + VOUT × (1 − VOUT ÷ VIN,MAX) ÷ (2 × fSW × L). And the winding resistance (DCR) and core loss at 2.3 MHz should be low enough for the efficiency you need. The datasheet gives no DCR target for this part.

### Output capacitor

Use X7R or better ceramic capacitors. The datasheet recommends two or three 10 µF low-ESR capacitors in parallel, depending on the input and output voltage: its table calls for three for outputs from 0.6 V to 1.0 V, and two for 1.1 V and above. The reference circuit uses two. The datasheet gives no ripple formulas for this part. The worked example below uses the usual estimate for the capacitive part of the ripple, ΔIL ÷ (8 × COUT × fSW), and ignores ESR. Ceramic capacitance falls as the DC voltage across it rises, so measured ripple is usually higher; check the effective capacitance on the capacitor's DC-bias curve.

### Input capacitor

The RMS current in the input capacitor is IOUT × √(D × (1 − D)), at most IOUT ÷ 2 at 50% duty. The capacitance needed for a given input ripple is CIN = IOUT × VOUT × (VIN − VOUT) ÷ (ΔVIN × fSW × Eff × VIN²), where ΔVIN is the allowed input ripple and Eff the efficiency. As an example with assumptions of our own (5 V in, 1.2 V out, 1 A, 50 mV of ripple, 90% efficiency), that asks for about 1.8 µF. The datasheet recommends a 10 µF low-ESR X7R or better ceramic for most applications, placed close to the IN and GND pins with the smallest possible loop, which leaves a wide margin once DC-bias loss is allowed for. The reference circuit adds a 47 µF electrolytic, CIN1; the datasheet doesn't say why, but bulk capacitance like this is the usual way to damp ringing when the input arrives over long leads.

### Feedforward, power good, enable and mode

The compensation is built in. A ceramic feedforward capacitor CFF in parallel with RTOP can improve the load-transient response, and the datasheet recommends it where the load steps are large. The datasheet's table uses 22 pF for every output except 0.6 V, where it isn't fitted.

The reference circuit connects EN and MODE straight to the VIN node. That conflicts with the datasheet's own layout section, which says it is not recommended to connect control signals directly to VIN, and that a resistor of 1 kΩ to 1 MΩ should be used if the lines are pulled high. A resistor in series with each pin follows the rule. For power-save operation, tie MODE to ground instead. EN is guaranteed high above 1.2 V and low below 0.4 V, and the datasheet gives no typical threshold or hysteresis, so don't use it as a precise voltage monitor.

> **The ripple numbers are small because the frequency is high.** At 2.3 MHz even a 1 µH inductor gives about 40% ripple, and 20 µF of output capacitance holds the capacitive ripple near 1 mV. In practice ESR and DC-bias loss will add to it.

## Layout

Silergy gives these layout rules, along with a suggested placement drawing (Figure 10 in the datasheet). In its drawing the pins are IN 1, EN 2, MODE 3, NC 4, FB 5, PG 6, LX 7, GND 8.

- Put the **input capacitors** as close as possible to the IN and GND pins, with the smallest loop, joined by a wide copper plane.
- Connect the **output capacitors'** negative terminals to the GND pin with wide copper, not vias.
- Place **RTOP, RBOT and CFF** as close to the FB pin as possible and route the feedback line away from LX and other fast signals. Take the feedback from the output capacitor with a Kelvin connection, not from the inductor's output terminal.
- Keep the **LX copper** small to limit EMI, but wide enough to keep its resistance and inductance low.
- Don't connect **control signals** (EN, MODE) directly to VIN; use a resistor between 1 kΩ and 1 MΩ.
- Put an adequate number of **ground vias** around the device, and maximize the copper connected to the GND pin. A ground plane is strongly recommended.

The datasheet's thermal resistance (θJA) is 94 °C/W on a 1 oz two-layer board and 70 °C/W on a 1 oz four-layer board, which correspond to 1.06 W and 1.7 W of power dissipation at 25 °C ambient. The junction-temperature rating runs to 150 °C, in line with the AEC-Q100 Grade 1 qualification.

## What the datasheet shows

Silergy's typical-performance pages cover this circuit at 25 °C and 5 V in, with L = 1 µH and COUT = 20 µF. They include efficiency against load current from 1 mA to 1 A for 1.2 V, 1.8 V and 3.3 V outputs at 3.3 V, 5 V and 6 V in, each in both PWM and PFM mode, start-up and shut-down from both VIN and EN, short-circuit captures from no load and from 1 A that show the hiccup behavior, output ripple, load-transient steps, and Bode plots. The Bode plots, taken at 1.2 V out and 1 A in PWM mode, show a crossover of about 167 kHz with 54° of phase margin at 3.3 V in, and about 182 kHz with 52° at 5 V in.

Reading the 1.2 V out, 5 V in curves by eye: in PFM mode, efficiency is about 75% at 1 mA, about 86–87% from 10 mA to 0.1 A, with a small dip to about 85% near 0.15 A, about 91% near 0.5 A, and 89–90% at 1 A. In forced PWM mode, it is about 5% at 1 mA, about 25% at 10 mA, about 60–65% at 0.1 A, and about 90% at 0.5–1 A. For 3.3 V out from 5 V in, both modes reach about 95–96% at 1 A. Treat these as rough, about two points either way, since they come from a graph.

## Things to watch

- **The reference circuit runs in forced PWM, which is poor at light load.** MODE is tied to VIN, so the circuit as drawn is the "PWM" case in the plots: about 5% efficient at 1 mA and about 25% at 10 mA, against about 75% and 87% in PFM. If the load idles at low current, tie MODE to ground (through a resistor if you want it switchable) to get power-save mode.
- **EN and MODE tied directly to VIN breaks the datasheet's own layout rule.** The layout section says not to connect control signals directly to VIN, and to use 1 kΩ to 1 MΩ if they are pulled high. Follow the rule, not the schematic.
- **The input range is narrow and the headroom is small.** The part runs from 2.8 V to 6 V and the absolute maximum on IN is 6.5 V, so a 5 V rail with a loose tolerance or transients leaves little room. This is a point-of-load part for an already regulated rail.
- **The switching frequency moves.** With spread spectrum, the frequency is a band around 2.3 MHz, not a single line. Make sure any filter or measurement that depends on the switching frequency allows for ±8%.
- **Know which variant you are buying.** The SA25901BAT discharges the output when disabled and the SA25901ABAT does not. If your load needs the output to fall at a known rate, or to sit near 0 V when off, choose the BAT version.
- **The minimum on-time limits low outputs at high input voltage.** The part's minimum on-time is 60 ns. At 6 V in and 1.2 V out the on-time is about 87 ns, so there is room. But the datasheet's table includes 0.6 V and 0.8 V outputs, and those need an on-time shorter than 60 ns above about 4.3 V in (0.6 V) and 5.8 V in (0.8 V), from VIN = VOUT ÷ (60 ns × 2.3 MHz). The datasheet doesn't say what the part does there, so ask Silergy before using those rows across the full input range.
- **The output range tops out at 5.5 V**, even though the input goes to 6 V, so this isn't a drop-in for a rail near its input.
- **Don't leave EN or MODE floating.** Both pins need a defined level.
