## How it works

The SQ52104E is a fixed-gain amplifier that sits across a shunt resistor on the supply side of a load. It measures VSENSE = VIN+ − VIN−, multiplies it by 200 and puts the result on OUT, referred to ground:

OUT = 200 × VSENSE

Current in the shunt makes VSENSE, so the output is a ground-referenced voltage that follows the load current and can go straight to a microcontroller's ADC.

**One pin for the supply and the positive input.** The package has four bumps (IN+, IN−, GND and OUT), so there is no separate supply pin. The datasheet says IN+ also serves as the power supply, which has three consequences:

- The common-mode range is the supply range, 1.8 V to 5.5 V. The datasheet says this is why the part cannot be used as a low-side monitor.
- The rail you measure is the rail that powers the part, so it must stay in that window while it is measured. Rails above 5.5 V, such as a 12 V bus, are out of range.
- The output can only go as high as the rail allows, so the usable output range shrinks as the rail drops (see the first calculated table). The datasheet's swing to the supply rail is within 0.1 V to 0.3 V of the rail with a 10 kΩ load.

**Zero-drift input stage.** The shunt voltage is a few millivolts at most here, so the offset limits the low end. The datasheet gives ±32 µV maximum for the SQ52104E (±5 µV typical) with 0.1 µV/°C maximum drift. The maximum gain error is ±0.16% (±0.02% typical).

**Speed.** The bandwidth is 2.5 kHz at gain 200 (5 kHz at gain 100) and the slew rate is 0.03 V/µs. Both gains fall on the same curve above a few kHz in the datasheet's gain plot, which is what a fixed gain-bandwidth product gives: 100 V/V × 5 kHz and 200 V/V × 2.5 kHz are both 500 kHz (calculated here). So this part trades speed for gain, and it is a slow monitor, not an overcurrent detector. The turn-on time is 200 µs typical.

**Two versions.** The SQ52104 (100 V/V) and SQ52104E (200 V/V) share the datasheet, the package and the pinout. The E version has the higher gain, the lower offset and gain error, and half the bandwidth. See the first design table.

### Choosing the shunt

The shunt is set by what the output can do, not by the input range, because the datasheet gives no input range for this part.

- **Output limit.** OUT must stay about 0.3 V below the rail, so the highest shunt voltage is (V+ − 0.3 V) ÷ 200: 7.5 mV at 1.8 V, 15 mV at 3.3 V and 19.5 mV at 4.2 V (first calculated table, which also gives the 100 V/V values). A battery that runs from 3.0 V to 4.2 V therefore sets the range by its lowest voltage: 13.5 mV.
- **Offset.** The ±32 µV maximum offset is the fixed error, and it is a bigger fraction of a smaller shunt voltage. The second calculated table shows each shunt as a full-scale current, a dissipation and the offset as a current.
- **Dissipation.** A larger shunt gives a larger signal and less offset error, and costs more heat and more voltage lost in the rail. At gain 200 the signal is large already, so the shunt can be small: a 10 mΩ shunt covers about 5 mA to 1.5 A on a 3.3 V rail and loses 23 mW at full scale.

### Supply bypass and the shunt connection

The datasheet recommends a 0.1 µF MLCC as close as possible to the IN+ and GND pins. Because the supply is the sense line, this capacitor sits on the monitored rail, on the supply side of the shunt. The bill of materials has a 100 nF, 50 V, X7R in 0603. Take the input pins from the shunt with a Kelvin connection (see Layout).

### Input filtering

The datasheet's position is that an input filter in front of the part is not recommended, but it can be done. The reason is the currents into the pins: about 18 µA into IN+ (the supply current, which flows through the pin) and about 3 µA into IN−. A resistor in each input drops a different voltage, and the difference subtracts from the shunt voltage. The datasheet's example is a 10 Ω resistor in each input with a 0.1 µF capacitor between the inputs (its Figure 5) and a 40 mV shunt voltage, and it says the extra error can easily exceed the 0.18% maximum gain error.

The calculated table above shows the size for the E version, with the typical currents: 150 µV for 10 Ω in each input, which is 1.0% of a 15 mV signal and 3.0% of 5 mV. That is large, because the E version's signals are small, so the filter resistors should be kept to a few ohms or left out. The corner for 10 Ω and 0.1 µF is about 80 kHz, far above the amplifier's own 2.5 kHz bandwidth, so such a filter only removes noise ahead of the amplifier and doesn't set the response (calculated here). Any current drawn from OUT also enters through IN+ (the datasheet says the output current flows through that pin too), so with 10 Ω each microamp of output load adds 10 µV of error: a 10 kΩ load at 3 V (300 µA) would add 3 mV, more than the whole 15 mV signal (our arithmetic). The datasheet's remedy for a filter that costs too much accuracy is to filter after the amplifier, with a buffer if a low output impedance is needed.

### Worked example: a one-cell battery rail, 0 to 1.3 A

These numbers are calculated here from the datasheet's values, and nothing was measured. The circuit is the application schematic: SQ52104E (gain 200), RSENSE = 10 mΩ, on the supply side of a load run from a lithium cell (3.0 V to 4.2 V).

- **Range.** The highest output is the rail minus 0.3 V, so at 3.0 V it is 2.7 V, which is 1.35 A at 2 V per amp. Take 1.3 A as the top of the range: 13 mV across the shunt and 2.6 V on OUT. At a full cell (4.2 V) the same output corresponds to the same 1.3 A, and the output could go as high as 3.9 V (1.95 A).
- **Transfer.** OUT = 200 × 10 mΩ × I = 2 V per amp.
- **Dissipation.** 17 mW in the shunt at 1.3 A, and 13 mV lost from the rail.

The error depends on the current, because the offset is fixed and the gain error is a percentage:

| Current | VSENSE | OUT (ideal) | Offset error (32 µV) | Offset and gain error (0.16%) | With the 1% shunt tolerance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 mA | 0.1 mV | 20 mV | 32% | 32% | 33% |
| 25 mA | 0.25 mV | 50 mV | 12.8% | 13.0% | 14.0% |
| 50 mA | 0.5 mV | 0.10 V | 6.4% | 6.6% | 7.6% |
| 0.1 A | 1 mV | 0.20 V | 3.2% | 3.4% | 4.4% |
| 0.25 A | 2.5 mV | 0.50 V | 1.28% | 1.44% | 2.44% |
| 0.5 A | 5 mV | 1.00 V | 0.64% | 0.80% | 1.80% |
| 1.0 A | 10 mV | 2.00 V | 0.32% | 0.48% | 1.48% |
| 1.3 A | 13 mV | 2.60 V | 0.25% | 0.41% | 1.41% |

These are worst-case sums of the datasheet's maximum values at 25 °C. They leave out the terms below, the ADC's own error and the nonlinearity (±0.01% typical), and in practice errors rarely all add in the same direction. With the typical offset of ±5 µV the offset column is one sixth as large. Two things stand out: below about 0.25 A the offset sets the result, and from 0.5 A up the 1% shunt (the bill of materials part) is the largest single error.

**Terms left out above (our arithmetic):**

- **Temperature.** The offset limit is given at 25 °C. At the maximum drift of 0.1 µV/°C, 100 °C adds up to 10 µV, so the offset could reach ±42 µV at 125 °C, which is 4.2 mA at 10 mΩ. The gain drift of 1.5 ppm/°C maximum adds up to 0.015% over the same 100 °C.
- **Rail voltage.** The rail is both the common-mode voltage and the supply, so both rejection terms act on one pin. The CMRR and PSRR are 100 dB minimum, which is 10 µV/V each, so a 1.2 V change in the rail (from the 4.2 V test point to 3.0 V) moves the offset by up to 12 µV for each term, or 24 µV if they add. The datasheet doesn't say whether they add.

## Layout

The datasheet's three rules, with the layout of its Figure 8 (top layer only):

- **Use a Kelvin connection from IN+ and IN− to the shunt.** With a few milliohms, the resistance of the traces between the shunt pads and the amplifier pins is large next to the shunt, and it adds an error. A Kelvin connection (the two input traces taken from the shunt's own pads, not from the high-current trace) means only the shunt's resistance is measured.
- **Keep the loop formed by those two traces small.**
- **Put the 0.1 µF MLCC bypass capacitor as close as possible to IN+ and GND.**

In the figure, the four bumps sit in a 2 × 2 grid (A2 IN− and B2 OUT above, A1 IN+ and B1 GND below), the shunt runs across the left side with its upper pad to IN− and its lower pad to IN+, and the capacitor sits under the part, between the IN+ copper and the GND copper.

## Things to watch

- **It cannot do low-side sensing, and the rail must be 1.8 V to 5.5 V.** IN+ is the supply, as the datasheet states. A 12 V bus or a 24 V rail is out, and so is a rail that dips below 1.8 V while you still want a reading.
- **The usable output range depends on the rail.** OUT stops about 0.3 V under the supply, so at gain 200 the shunt voltage cannot exceed 7.5 mV at 1.8 V or 15 mV at 3.3 V. A battery's low end sets the range. See the first calculated table.
- **The high gain makes the input range small.** At 200 V/V, 20 mV of shunt voltage already gives 4 V. If you want a larger shunt voltage (less offset error), the 100 V/V version doubles the range for the same rail.
- **The output drive is weak.** The swing is specified into 10 kΩ to ground. The swing plot (by eye, SQ52104WMS) shows the sourcing current limiting at about 2 mA at −40 °C, 5 mA at 25 °C and 7 mA at 125 °C, with the output voltage falling well before that. The datasheet has no output current or short-circuit figure in the table. Drive an ADC input or a high-impedance buffer, and since the output current flows through IN+, anything drawn from OUT also adds to the supply current.
- **Filters in the inputs cost accuracy.** See the filter table: 10 Ω in each input is about 1% error at 15 mV across the shunt.
- **The offset moves with the rail.** The rail is the common mode and the supply at once, and CMRR and PSRR are each 100 dB minimum, so a rail that moves from 3.0 V to 4.2 V changes the offset by up to 12 to 24 µV against a 32 µV limit.
- **The bandwidth is 2.5 kHz at gain 200.** A single pole at 2.5 kHz has a rise time of about 140 µs (0.35 ÷ bandwidth, calculated here), and the datasheet's turn-on time is 200 µs. Don't use it for fast current limiting.
- **Shunt tolerance is the largest error from half an amp up.** The bill of materials shunt is 1%, which is six times the amplifier's gain error. The datasheet gives no shunt temperature coefficient (our note).
- **The plots are for the 100 V/V part.** The gain, noise and swing plots were measured on the SQ52104WMS. Where the two versions differ (the bandwidth is half, the offset smaller), use the table.
- **The thermal figures hardly matter.** At about 18 µA from a 4.2 V rail the part dissipates under 0.1 mW, against the 0.32 W package rating (θJA 315 °C/W on a single-layer board).

## Datasheet notes

- **The first-page feature list gives the SQ52104 values.** It lists ±7 µV typical offset, 0.2 µV/°C maximum offset drift, 0.06% typical gain error and 1 ppm/°C maximum gain drift, which are the 100 V/V part's numbers. The SQ52104E's are in the table (±5 µV, 0.1 µV/°C, 0.02%, 1.5 ppm/°C).
- **The output swing to the rail has a Typ value of (V+) − 0.1 V and a Max value of (V+) − 0.3 V.** A maximum that is lower than the typical value suggests the two columns are the typical and minimum swing. We read it as the output reaching at least 0.3 V below the rail, and use that in the calculations.
- **Three different CMRR figures.** The table says 120 dB typical. The CMRR-against-temperature plot reads about 1.4 to 1.8 µV/V (by eye), which is 115 to 117 dB. The CMRR-against-frequency plot has its vertical axis labelled "Gain (dB)" and reads about 108 dB at low frequency (by eye).
- **The two quiescent-current plots differ by about 1 µA** (about 17 µA against temperature and about 18 µA against VSENSE, by eye), and the temperature plot doesn't give VSENSE. The table's typical is 18 µA.
- **The common-mode row of the absolute maximum and recommended operating tables is labelled "VIN+ − VIN−".** That is the label of a differential voltage, but the values (GND − 0.3 V to 5.5 V and 1.8 V to 5.5 V) are a common-mode voltage. The header of the absolute maximum table also reads "Note 1)Note 1)".
- **The input bias current has no limits and no sign.** The table gives 3 µA typical for IN− only, and doesn't say which way it flows.
- **Missing µ symbols.** In the electrical table the units read "V" for the offset, "V/°C" for the drift, "A" for the bias current and "s" for the turn-on time, and the plots have "Offset Voltage ( V)" and "V/ s" for the slew rate. We read them all as µ.
