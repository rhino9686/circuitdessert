## How it works

The SY26533 is a peak-current-mode boost converter. Each 1 MHz cycle starts by turning the internal switch on, and the current in L1 rises. The switch turns off when that current reaches a threshold set by the error amplifier, and the inductor current then flows through the Schottky diode D1 into the output capacitor and the load. The error amplifier compares the FB pin with the 1.24 V reference, and an internal slope compensation avoids subharmonic oscillation at duty cycles above 50%. The loop compensation is inside the chip, so the circuit has no compensation parts.

**The output sets the duty cycle.** In steady state, an ideal boost converter gives D = (VOUT − VIN) ÷ VOUT. For 5 V to 12 V that is 58%, and for 4.5 V to 30 V it is 85%. The datasheet gives a maximum duty cycle of 85% minimum and 90% typical, which limits how large the ratio VOUT ÷ VIN can be.

**There is no switch on the output.** D1 is an external diode, so when the switch is off there is a path from VIN through L1 and D1 to the output. The output can't be regulated below VIN minus a diode drop, and the input can't be disconnected from the output with EN (our reading; the datasheet doesn't say).

**Light load.** The datasheet says the part operates in burst mode at light load: DCM starts when the inductor current falls to zero, and as the load falls the switching frequency falls to keep the output close to the target.

**Overcurrent and heat.** When the inductor current reaches the limit, the switch turns off for that cycle, and the limit resets at the start of the next one. The output voltage then falls as a function of the load. When the junction reaches 150 °C the part shuts down, and when it has cooled by about 10 °C it restarts with a full soft-start.

**Start-up.** A built-in soft start limits the rise rate of the output and the inrush current. With a large capacitance on the output, the datasheet says the inrush through the inductor can be very high, and it recommends a Schottky diode from the input to the output (DBYP in its figure), or an RC delay on EN (REN and CEN).

## Example application

The datasheet's typical design: a boost converter that takes 5–12 V to 12 V at 0.18 A, with a switch and a pull-down resistor on EN. Besides the IC it needs an inductor, a Schottky diode, a feedback divider and two ceramic capacitors, and nothing for the loop compensation. The datasheet calls the 0.18 A the "output current limit" and gives no output current rating. A second example on the same page doubles a 3–4.2 V input to 50 V with a diode and capacitor charge pump, covered at the end of this section.

### Feedback divider (R1, R2)

VOUT = 1.24 V × (R1 ÷ R2 + 1), so with R1 chosen, R2 = (R1 × 1.24 V) ÷ (VOUT − 1.24 V). The datasheet recommends 10 kΩ to 100 kΩ for both resistors, with large values for low power at light load. The table gives R2 for R1 = 100 kΩ, rounded to the nearest 1% value, with the output you get at the typical reference and over the reference range (1.21–1.265 V).

The second table under *Recommended components* above gives R2 for R1 = 100 kΩ at seven outputs from 5 V to 33 V, rounded to the nearest 1% value, with the output you get at the typical reference and over the reference range (1.21–1.265 V), and the current the divider draws. Those are calculated here, not from the datasheet.

The bill of materials uses R1 = 105 kΩ and R2 = 12 kΩ, which gives 12.09 V at the typical reference (11.80–12.33 V over its range). Both are our calculations.

> **The "10–100 kΩ for both" advice only reaches 13.6 V.** With R2 at its smallest recommended value of 10 kΩ and R1 at its largest of 100 kΩ, the output is 1.24 V × 11 = 13.6 V. Anything higher needs R2 below 10 kΩ, as the datasheet's own 50 V example does with 2.55 kΩ. The divider then draws more current, which counts against light-load efficiency (at 12 V it is already about 108 µA, similar to the 100 µA quiescent current).

### Inductor (L1)

The datasheet suggests a ripple current of about 40% of the maximum average input current:

**L = (VIN ÷ VOUT)² × (VOUT − VIN) ÷ (fSW × IOUT_MAX × 40%)**, where IOUT_MAX is the maximum load current. This is the ideal case, with an efficiency of 1 and the ripple at 40% of the input current. The datasheet says the part tolerates other ripple currents, so the final value can differ from the result.

Three conditions apply to the choice:

1. **Ripple.** The formula above, with the 40% target.
2. **Saturation current.** It must be above the peak inductor current at full load: ISAT,MIN > (VOUT ÷ (VIN × η)) × IOUT_MAX + VIN × (VOUT − VIN) ÷ (2 × fSW × L × VOUT). The datasheet uses the same peak for the diode, written as (VOUT ÷ (VIN × η)) × IOUT_MAX + ΔIL ÷ 2 with ΔIL = VIN × D ÷ (fSW × L). Because the switch limit can be as high as 925 mA, an inductor rated above that keeps it out of saturation in a fault (our suggestion).
3. **Resistance.** The datasheet suggests a DCR under 50 mΩ for good efficiency.

For 0.18 A out at 12 V, the ideal inductance depends on where VIN is, and it peaks near 8 V:

| VIN | L for 40% ripple | Ripple with 22 µH | As a share of the input current |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 V | 16.9 µH | 133 mA | 31% |
| 6 V | 20.8 µH | 136 mA | 38% |
| 8 V | 24.7 µH | 121 mA | 45% |
| 10 V | 19.3 µH | 76 mA | 35% |
| 11 V | 11.7 µH | 42 mA | 21% |

With the datasheet's 22 µH the ripple is between 21% and 45% of the input current from 5 to 11 V. It is over the 40% target only between about 6.3 V and 9.5 V, and peaks at 45% near 8 V, so 22 µH is a reasonable single value for the whole range. These are calculated here, with an efficiency of 1 in the share column.

### Output diode (D1)

The datasheet recommends a Schottky diode, for the low forward voltage and fast recovery. Its reverse voltage must be above the highest output voltage. Its peak current is the peak inductor current, and the datasheet says that a diode that can carry 1 A continuously is right for most circuits. The bill of materials has an SS34 (3 A, 40 V), which covers outputs up to about 33 V with some margin. The average current in D1 is the load current.

### Output capacitor (C4)

The datasheet recommends a ceramic X5R or better, rated at 50 V, with more than 10 µF, and sizes it for the ripple. When the switch is on, the load current comes from the capacitor alone, so for a ripple ΔVOUT:

**COUT ≥ ILOAD × D ÷ (ΔVOUT × fSW)**, and the ESR adds ΔVESR = ILOAD × RESR.

For 0.18 A at 5 V to 12 V (D = 58%):

| Ripple target | Minimum capacitance |
| --- | --- |
| 100 mV | 1.05 µF |
| 50 mV | 2.1 µF |
| 25 mV | 4.2 µF |
| 10 mV | 10.5 µF |

So the 10 µF in the datasheet's bill of materials gives about 10 mV of capacitive ripple at the full 0.18 A, from nominal capacitance. Ceramic capacitance falls with DC bias, and a 1206 X7R part at 25 V loses a lot of it at 12 V, so the real value is lower (our note; check the maker's DC-bias curve). The ESR term in the datasheet uses the load current, but the charge flows through the capacitor at the diode's peak current, so a real ESR step is larger than ILOAD × RESR (our observation).

### Input capacitor (C1)

The datasheet gives a voltage rating of at least 20% above the maximum input, X5R or X7R, and one 10 µF X5R capacitor is recommended for most circuits. For an input of up to 30 V that means a rating of at least 36 V, so a 50 V part. When the supply is connected through long wires, it recommends adding a bulk capacitor (electrolytic, tantalum or polymer) to damp the overshoot and ringing from the wire inductance.

### Enable (K, R3)

EN is high above 1.5 V and low below 0.4 V. The datasheet says not to leave it floating. In the typical circuit, a switch K connects EN to VIN, and R3 (1 MΩ) pulls it to ground when the switch is open. With the switch closed, R3 draws VIN ÷ 1 MΩ, which is 5 µA at 5 V and 12 µA at 12 V. The layout notes add that if the chip that drives EN is high-impedance in shutdown, and IN is wired straight to a Li-Ion cell, the 1 MΩ pull-down stops noise from turning the regulator on.

### Worked example: 5 V to 12 V at 0.18 A

These numbers are calculated here from the datasheet's values and the efficiency graph, and nothing was measured. The datasheet's graph shows an efficiency of about 91% at 0.18 A with VIN = 5 V and VOUT = 12 V.

**Duty cycle and currents.**

| Quantity | Value | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duty cycle | 58% | (12 − 5) ÷ 12 |
| Input current | 0.475 A | 0.18 × 12 ÷ (5 × 0.91) |
| Ripple (22 µH) | 133 mA | 5 × 0.583 ÷ (1 MHz × 22 µH) |
| Peak inductor and diode current | 0.54 A | 0.475 + 0.133 ÷ 2 |
| Output power | 2.16 W | 12 × 0.18 |
| Loss | 0.21 W | 2.16 ÷ 0.91 − 2.16 |

The peak current is already above the switch's guaranteed minimum limit of 450 mA, and well below the maximum of 925 mA. The next section turns that around.

**How much current the switch can deliver.**

The load current at the limit is IOUT = (ILIM − ΔIL ÷ 2) × VIN × η ÷ VOUT. The table uses 22 µH, VOUT = 12 V and η = 0.9, for the three limits in the datasheet (450, 750 and 925 mA).

| VIN | Duty cycle | Ripple | IOUT at 450 mA | IOUT at 750 mA | IOUT at 925 mA |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3.3 V | 73% | 109 mA | 98 mA | 172 mA | 215 mA |
| 5 V | 58% | 133 mA | 144 mA | 256 mA | 322 mA |
| 8 V | 33% | 121 mA | 234 mA | 414 mA | 519 mA |
| 10 V | 17% | 76 mA | 309 mA | 534 mA | 665 mA |

> **At 5 V in, the 0.18 A design needs more than the guaranteed minimum current limit.** The limit is 450 mA minimum, and the part delivers about 144 mA at that limit and about 256 mA at the typical one. A part at the low end of the window can't supply 0.18 A at 5 V, and at 3.3 V in even the typical limit gives only 172 mA. The datasheet gives the limit only at 55% duty, and the duty cycle in these rows runs from 17% to 73%, so treat the other rows as estimates. The datasheet doesn't say how the limit changes with duty cycle.

**Heat.**

The datasheet gives θJA = 161 °C/W on a 2 in × 2 in FR-4 board, and a power dissipation of 0.6 W at 25 °C, which is about (125 − 25) ÷ 161.

- **The whole circuit** loses 0.21 W at 91%. Most of that is outside the chip: the diode, the inductor's resistance and the switching. If all of it landed in the package, the rise would be 34 °C, so this is an upper bound.
- **The switch alone**, with an RMS current of about 0.36 A (0.475 A × √0.58), loses 0.36² × 0.4 Ω = 53 mW at the typical on-resistance and 79 mW at the 600 mΩ maximum, a rise of 8 to 13 °C on that board. Switching loss comes on top.

**Output voltage tolerance.** With R1 = 105 kΩ and R2 = 12 kΩ the output is 12.09 V at the typical reference, and 11.80–12.33 V over the datasheet's VREF range, before the resistor tolerance (1% resistors add up to about ±1.8% on the output in the worst case).

**Large input voltages and the minimum on-time.** The minimum on-time is 100 ns typical and 150 ns maximum, 10–15% of a 1 MHz period. For a 12 V output, the ideal duty cycle drops below 15% when VIN is above 10.2 V (the 150 ns maximum), and below 10% when it is above 10.8 V (the 100 ns typical). The datasheet's input range of 5–12 V includes this region, and at the top the output can't be regulated by an ideal boost converter. The datasheet doesn't say how the part behaves there, so check it on a bench, with the output ripple and the output voltage in mind (our observation).

### Charge-pump example: 3–4.2 V to 50 V

The datasheet's second application example (page 10) adds a diode and capacitor stage to a boost converter, and takes a Li-Ion input to 50 V. The parts are L1 = 33 µH, C1 = 4.7 µF/10 V, C2 = 100 nF/50 V, C3 = 1 µF/50 V, D1 and D3 = 1N4148, D2 = SS14, R1 = 100 kΩ, R2 = 2.55 kΩ and C4 = 100 nF/100 V. The datasheet gives no output current rating, no efficiency figure and no explanation of the circuit, only oscilloscope captures at 3.7 V in with a 10 mA load, so what follows is our reading of the drawing.

- **How it appears to work.** D2 charges C3 from the LX node to about the LX peak voltage VPK each time the switch turns off. When the switch is on and LX is near ground, D3 charges C2 from C3. When LX rises again, C2 adds its voltage to LX, and D1 passes the sum to C4. That gives VOUT ≈ 2 × VPK − (the three diode drops).
- **Output voltage.** The feedback divider (100 kΩ over 2.55 kΩ) is on the final output, so the output is 1.24 V × (100 ÷ 2.55 + 1) = 49.9 V at the typical reference, and 48.7–50.9 V over the range.
- **LX peak.** With about 1.6 V of diode drops (our assumption: 0.4 V for the SS14 and 0.6 V for each 1N4148), VPK ≈ (49.9 + 1.6) ÷ 2 = 25.7 V. That is under the 36 V rating of the LX pin, which is the reason the circuit works with a 33 V part.
- **Duty cycle.** For a 25.7 V peak, the ideal duty cycle is 84% at 4.2 V in and 88% at 3 V in. The guaranteed maximum duty cycle is 85%, and 90% is only typical, so at 3 V the part is above the guaranteed value, and the losses push it higher.
- **Divider load.** The divider alone draws 49.9 V ÷ 102.55 kΩ = 0.49 mA from the output, 24 mW.

| Input | Ideal duty cycle (VPK = 25.7 V) | Against the 85% minimum of DMAX |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 4.2 V | 84% | just inside |
| 3.7 V | 86% | above |
| 3.0 V | 88% | above |

## Layout

Silergy gives these rules, with two example boards (top and bottom layers) for the boost and charge-pump circuits.

- Place **CIN, L, R1 and R2 close to the IC**.
- Connect the ground pin to **as much copper as the board allows**, ideally a ground plane or a large pour, for heat and for lower switching noise.
- Place **CIN close to the IN and GND pins**, and keep the loop it forms with the ground pin as small as possible.
- **Minimize the copper area on the LX pin.**
- **Keep R1, R2 and the FB trace away from the LX net**, to avoid crosstalk.
- If the chip that drives EN is high-impedance in shutdown and IN is wired directly to a power source such as a Li-Ion cell, **add a 1 MΩ pull-down from EN to ground.**

Pin 1 is PGND, pin 2 GND, pin 3 FB, pin 4 EN, pin 5 IN and pin 6 LX.

## Things to watch

- **The 0.18 A design isn't guaranteed at 5 V in.** The minimum switch limit is 450 mA, and at 5 V to 12 V that supports about 144 mA, and about 256 mA at the typical limit (see the worked example). The datasheet calls the 0.18 A the "output current limit" and gives no output current rating.
- **The datasheet's own 30 V example exceeds the minimum limit.** It gets a peak diode current of 0.504 A for a 50 mA load at 4.5 V in, which is above the 450 mA minimum switch limit (given at 55% duty, where the example runs at 85%). At the minimum limit the example's load is about 44 mA (our calculation), not 50 mA. The example's D = 0.85 is the ideal value; with the 0.8 efficiency it would be about 0.88.
- **The resistor advice reaches only 13.6 V.** "10 kΩ to 100 kΩ for both resistors" can't give more than 1.24 V × 11 = 13.6 V, and the datasheet's own 50 V example uses R2 = 2.55 kΩ.
- **It is not a buck-boost.** With the input at or above the output, a boost converter with a diode can't regulate. The typical design lists a 5–12 V input for a 12 V output, and the minimum on-time (10–15% of the period) makes the ideal duty cycle unreachable above about 10.2 V in. Check the behaviour between 10 and 12 V before you rely on it.
- **The output stays connected to the input in shutdown.** L1 and D1 form a path, so with EN low the output sits about a diode drop below VIN, and the feedback divider draws current from it (our reading). The 5 µA shutdown current is the current into the IN pin.
- **The ratio has a ceiling.** The duty cycle is limited to 85% at worst and 90% typically, so the ideal output is at most 6.7 times VIN, or 10 times at the typical value, and less with the losses. From 3 V that is 20 V (or 30 V), and 33 V needs at least 5 V in at the minimum duty cycle (3.3 V at the typical).
- **The charge-pump example is at the edge of the duty-cycle limit.** The ideal duty cycle at the 25.7 V peak is 84–88% over its 3–4.2 V input, against 85% minimum and 90% typical (see the worked example). It also has no stated output current. The 50 V output is the final stage, and the LX pin only sees about 26 V.
- **The output capacitor's voltage rating is given two ways.** The text recommends a 50 V rating and more than 10 µF, and the bill of materials has 10 µF at 25 V for a 12 V output. A 25 V part is fine for 12 V on paper, but its capacitance at bias is lower, and for outputs above 20 V the 25 V part is not an option.
- **The minimum off-time is given two ways.** The feature list says 100 ns typical, and the electrical table says 10% (typical) and 15% (maximum) of the switching period, which is 100–150 ns at 1 MHz.
- **The feedback resistors differ between figures.** The page 1 figure uses 100 kΩ and 11.5 kΩ, and the page 10 bill of materials 105 kΩ and 12 kΩ. Both give about 12 V (12.02 V and 12.09 V at the typical reference).
- **The EN and UVLO limits are one-sided.** The EN rising threshold is only a minimum (1.5 V), and the falling threshold is only a maximum (0.4 V), so the guaranteed switching window is below 0.4 V and above 1.5 V and nothing in between. The UVLO has both a minimum and a maximum (1.3–2.2 V) and no hysteresis.
- **Units have dropped out of the PDF.** The quiescent and shutdown currents print as "A", and "33 H", "4.7 F" and "1 F" appear in the charge-pump figure. They are read as µA, µH and µF, as the text and the product page agree.
- **The LX margin at the top of the output range is small.** The absolute maximum on LX is 36 V and the output goes up to 33 V, so there is about 3 V of margin over the output, less the diode drop (our arithmetic).
- **The revision label is mixed.** The footer says Rev. 1.0, and the revision history lists Revision 1.0 (April 20, 2023, language improvements) and 0.9 (June 20, 2021, initial release), while the page footer carries ©2021.
