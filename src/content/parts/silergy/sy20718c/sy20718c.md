## How it works

The SY20718C does two jobs with one set of switches. The adapter goes in at VIN, the output port hangs off SYS, and the battery connects at the far side of the inductor, so energy can flow either way. When an adapter is present it runs as a synchronous **buck charger** from the input to the battery. With no adapter, and boost enabled, it runs as a synchronous **boost converter** from the battery to SYS. No microcontroller is needed: an internal state machine picks the mode, runs the charge stages, drives the status LED and handles faults.

Between VIN and SYS are two back-to-back N-channel power-path switches (called SA and SB in the datasheet, 80 mΩ together). They stop current leaking from SYS or the battery back into the adapter. On an input overvoltage they turn off along with the charger. The block diagram feeds the high-side switch from SYS, so when an adapter is present the SYS pin carries the adapter voltage through those switches. The datasheet doesn't discuss loads on SYS while charging, so if you plan one, count its current in the input budget.

**Charging** follows the usual Li-Ion profile, set by the battery voltage. Below about 2.0 V (1.9–2.1 V) the cell is treated as shorted. Below the trickle threshold of 2.6 V (2.5–2.7 V), with 200 mV of hysteresis on the way up, it gets a trickle current of 0.1 × ICC; the datasheet says the charger stops and latches off if it spends more than 2 hours in trickle mode, and the input has to be recycled to restart. Above that, the charger holds the constant current ICC set by RCHG until the cell reaches 4.2 V or 4.35 V, then holds that voltage while the current falls. Charging ends when the current falls to 0.1 × ICC, and the LED goes out. The charger stays in charging mode while the adapter remains plugged in, and recharges if the cell falls by about 100 mV (45–170 mV, specified at 4.35 V). The charger ramps its current up gradually when it starts. The datasheet's soft-start capture shows a rise over several milliseconds.

Two limits protect the adapter. The **input current limit**, set by RIIN, caps what the circuit draws from the adapter. The **input voltage limit** (VDPM) watches a divider from VIN: if the voltage drops to the internal 1.2 V reference, the circuit clamps VIN at the configured level, so the source isn't pulled down any further (the datasheet says the function protects the input source from high-current conditions). A separate input overcurrent protection works in hiccup mode, with a threshold 25% above the input current limit. The power-path switches turn off for about 190 ms and on for about 5 ms repeatedly until the fault clears.

**Boost** is enabled by pulling OTGB low (below 0.35 V typical) when no adapter is present, and disabled by pulling it high. The datasheet's scope capture of OTGB enabling and disabling shows switching only while the pin is held low, so treat it as a level, not a toggle. In the reference circuit R8 pulls OTGB up to the battery node, so boost is off until something pulls the pin low: a switch, a button circuit or the host. The datasheet doesn't show that part of the circuit. In boost mode SYS is held at 5.15 V and the current is limited by RSYS. If the SYS voltage rises 5% (3–7%) above its target, switching stops until it recovers. If the battery falls to about 2.5 V, the part stops, and the datasheet says it must be re-enabled after the fault clears. A short at SYS stops switching and puts the part into hiccup mode. With boost shut down (OTGB high), the part draws at most 20 µA from the battery.

In charging mode only, the OTGB pin doubles as the **battery temperature input**. A thermistor network pulls it down from the battery voltage. The charger stops when the pin rises above the cold-limit threshold (67.7% of the battery voltage, 65.7–69.7%) or falls below the hot-limit threshold (31.9%, 29.9–33.9%), and resumes by itself when it returns to the window. If the pin is above 90% of the battery voltage, the temperature function is off. Figure 1 on the datasheet's first page draws a thermistor (RNTC) beside the battery, but the application schematic and its bill of materials leave that position unfitted, so this reference circuit runs with the function off.

The **STAT** pin is an open-drain output that pulls an LED low. It is low while charging and while discharging, high when charge is complete, and flashes at 2 Hz on any fault (input overvoltage, battery overvoltage, SYS overvoltage, battery short, thermistor faults, thermal shutdown, timeout, or a short at SYS). Thermal shutdown is at 150 °C with 30 °C of hysteresis.

## Example application

A single-cell power bank built from the circuit in Silergy's datasheet. It charges one Li-Ion cell at up to 2 A from a 5 V adapter, and boosts the cell to 5.15 V at up to 2.5 A. One inductor and one set of switches do both jobs, a built-in state machine picks the mode, and a few resistors set the limits. This section keeps the datasheet's component values, adds the reasoning behind each one, and works the numbers for boost and charge.

### Current-setting resistors

Three resistors from the IIN, ICHG and ISYS pins to ground set the input current limit (RIIN), the charge current (RCHG) and the boost output current limit (RSYS). They sit on the top edge of the drawing, with one end at the pin and the other at ground. The datasheet gives each as a curve, with these exact points:

| Resistor | Datasheet point | Sets |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RCHG | 2.55 kΩ gives 2 A (±10%) | Constant charge current, ICC |
| RIIN | 0.75 kΩ gives 2.5 A (2.25–2.75 A), measured at 1 A charge | Input current limit in charging mode |
| RSYS | 2.2 kΩ gives 2.5 A (2.25–2.75 A) | Current limit in boost mode |

Each curve looks close to inverse proportion, and the datasheet's points fit that. We use it for the estimates in the "Other settings" section: ICC ≈ 5.1 V ÷ RCHG, ISYS ≈ 5.5 V ÷ RSYS and IIN limit ≈ 1.875 V ÷ RIIN, with the resistances in kΩ and the currents in amperes. These are our fits to the datasheet's points and graphs, not Silergy's formulas. They agree with the curves to within about 10%, with the graph reading slightly higher at the high-resistance end, so confirm the final current on the bench.

### Input voltage threshold (R2 and R3)

A divider from VIN to the VDPM pin sets the voltage below which the input is clamped: VIN,min = 1.2 V × (R2 + R3) ÷ R3. The reference circuit uses R2 = 36 kΩ and R3 = 13 kΩ, which gives 4.52 V. The reference is specified as 1.17–1.23 V, so the threshold lands between 4.41 V and 4.64 V before the resistors' own tolerance (5% in the BOM). The adapter-OK comparator drops out at about 4.3 V falling (the 4.5 V rising threshold less its 200 mV hysteresis, our subtraction), so choose a threshold above that and above the point where your adapter collapses under load. The charger then backs off before the input is lost.

### Cell voltage (CV1 and R19)

Leave the CV pin open or pull it low for a 4.2 V cell; pull it high for 4.35 V. The reference circuit uses a jumper, CV1, that either grounds the pin or connects it through R19 (10 kΩ) to SYS. For a fixed cell type, replace the jumper with the one connection. The datasheet gives no logic thresholds for the CV pin.

### Inductor

The datasheet bases the inductor on boost mode, where the inductor current is larger, and asks for a ripple of about 40% of the average inductor current. The minimum inductance is L = (VBAT ÷ VSYS)² × (VSYS − VBAT) ÷ (ISYS × fSW × 40%), where ISYS is the maximum discharge current. The result depends on the battery voltage. Setting its derivative to zero shows the worst case is at VBAT = ⅔ × VSYS = 3.43 V. For 5.15 V and 2.5 A at 500 kHz, that needs 1.53 µH, which matches the datasheet's recommended 1.5 µH. It adds that the part tolerates other ripple amplitudes, so the final value can differ a little.

Two more conditions apply. The saturation current has to exceed the peak inductor current at full load: ISAT > VSYS × ISYS ÷ VBAT + (VBAT ÷ VSYS) × (VSYS − VBAT) ÷ (2 × fSW × L). And the winding resistance (DCR) should be low; the datasheet recommends under 10 mΩ, and the core loss at 500 kHz also affects efficiency. The datasheet doesn't give the current rating of its inductor, the TDK SPM6530T-1R5M, so check it against the peak current in the worked example below.

### Capacitors

Use X5R or X7R ceramics. **CIN** on VIN needs 10 µF or more with a voltage rating above 16 V (see Things to watch). **CBAT**, at the battery node, should be 10 µF or more, rated above 10 V; the reference circuit adds a 1 µF capacitor, C3, beside it. **CSYS** on the output should be at least two capacitors of 22 µF or more, rated above 10 V. The datasheet gives no ripple formulas for the capacitors. The worked example below uses the usual estimates, ΔIL ÷ (8 × C × fSW) for the charger output and IOUT × D ÷ (fSW × C) for the boost output, which count only the capacitance and ignore ESR and ESL. Ceramic capacitance falls as the DC voltage across it rises, so real ripple is higher. Check each part's DC-bias curve.

The block diagram connects the high-side switch to SYS, behind the power-path switches, so in charging mode much of the chopped current comes out of CSYS, not CIN. The datasheet doesn't say how the current divides, so the worked example treats CSYS as the capacitor that carries it all, which is the conservative reading.

### Battery temperature network

The datasheet gives equations for two thermistor networks, and the reference circuit leaves the thermistor out. Choose the temperatures you want to stop charging at, look up the thermistor resistance at each (RUT at the cold limit and ROT at the hot limit), and calculate. For the first network (R1 from the battery node to OTGB, then R2 in series with the thermistor to ground), R2 = [KOT × (1 − KUT) × RUT − KUT × (1 − KOT) × ROT] ÷ (KUT − KOT) and R1 = (1 ÷ KOT − 1) × (R2 + ROT). With the typical thresholds, KUT = 67.7% and KOT = 31.9%, these reduce to R2 = 0.288 × RUT − 1.288 × ROT and R1 = 2.135 × (R2 + ROT). For the second network (the thermistor in parallel with R2), the typical-value equations are R2 = 0.358 × RUT × ROT ÷ (0.103 × RUT − 0.461 × ROT) and R1 = 0.477 × R2 × RUT ÷ (RUT + R2). The typical-value forms follow from the general equations; we checked the algebra. In the reference circuit's schematic, R8 (100 kΩ) is the pull-up from the battery node and R9 (not fitted) is below it; we read those as the R1 and R2 positions, but the datasheet doesn't label them that way. The worked example below runs the numbers for a 10 kΩ thermistor.

### Status LED, USB lines and snubber

The LED connects to STAT through R1 (5.1 kΩ), with its supply taken through the two Schottky diodes of D2 (BAT54C, common cathode) from the higher of SYS and the battery, so the LED works with or without the adapter. With a 2 V LED and a 0.3 V diode drop (our assumptions, the datasheet names neither), R1 gives about 0.5 mA from a 5 V supply and 0.3 mA from a 3.7 V battery, so choose a high-efficiency LED.

DP and DM go to the output port through a 0 Ω resistor (R7, R12) with a 5.1 V zener clamp to ground (D4, D3) on each line. The datasheet says only that the pins support BC1.2 handshaking and the Apple and Samsung protocols. We read the zeners as protection against a fault voltage on the port and the 0 Ω resistors as places to add filtering, but the datasheet doesn't say.

A snubber of C8 (10 nF) in series with R10 (2.2 Ω) goes from LX to ground. The datasheet doesn't discuss it, and its resistor is a larger 0805 size than the rest. As a check, a capacitor charged and discharged through a resistor each cycle dissipates C × V² × fSW whatever the resistance, which for 10 nF, 5.15 V and 500 kHz is 0.13 W (our calculation, assuming LX swings the full SYS voltage). See Things to watch.

### Worked example: 5 V adapter, one 4.2 V cell

These numbers are calculated here from the datasheet's formulas and its efficiency graphs. The datasheet doesn't list them, and nothing below was measured. They assume ideal switches and nominal component values, so real currents run higher by the amount of the losses.

#### Boost: battery to 5.15 V at 2.5 A

| Battery voltage | Duty cycle | Average inductor current | Ripple, 1.5 µH | Peak inductor current | SYS ripple, capacitive part |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2.5 V | 51% | 5.15 A | 1.72 A | 6.01 A | 58 mV |
| 3.0 V | 42% | 4.29 A | 1.67 A | 5.13 A | 47 mV |
| 3.7 V | 28% | 3.48 A | 1.39 A | 4.17 A | 32 mV |
| 4.2 V | 18% | 3.07 A | 1.03 A | 3.58 A | 21 mV |

> **The recommended inductance sits at the worst-case battery voltage.** The datasheet's formula peaks at 3.43 V, where it asks for 1.53 µH, so a 1.5 µH inductor gives a ripple of about 41% of the average current there instead of 40%. At 3.7 V the same formula gives 1.50 µH and at 3.0 V 1.46 µH.

> **Low battery voltage drives the inductor current.** At 2.5 V (where the datasheet's depletion threshold stops the part) the average inductor current is 5.2 A and the peak 6.0 A. The datasheet's recommended maximum continuous LX current is 6 A, and it gives 8 A as the typical peak switch current in discharge mode. Real currents are higher than these ideal values, by about the efficiency loss.

> **The SYS ripple here is the capacitive part only.** For 44 µF at 3.7 V it is 32 mV. The datasheet's 2 A scope capture shows more than that, so ESR, ESL and capacitance lost to DC bias add to it.

#### Charge: 5 V adapter, 2 A

| Battery voltage | Duty cycle | Ripple, 1.5 µH | Peak inductor current | Input RMS current | Input ripple, 44 µF | Battery ripple, 11 µF |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 3.0 V | 60% | 1.60 A | 2.80 A | 0.98 A | 22 mV | 36 mV |
| 3.7 V | 74% | 1.28 A | 2.64 A | 0.88 A | 17 mV | 29 mV |
| 4.2 V | 84% | 0.90 A | 2.45 A | 0.73 A | 12 mV | 20 mV |

The ripple is largest when the battery voltage is half the input voltage (2.5 V), but a cell that low is still in trickle charge at 0.1 × ICC, so the worst full-current case is just above the trickle threshold, near 3 V. The peak current stays well under the typical 4.5 A peak switch current the datasheet gives for charge mode.

#### Input current and power path

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Input power for 2 A into 4.2 V at about 91% efficiency | 9.2 W (efficiency read by eye from the datasheet's plot) |
| Input current at 5 V in | 1.85 A |
| Input current at 4.5 V in (bottom of the operating range) | 2.05 A, slightly above the 2 A recommended continuous VIN current |

> **Full charge current is at the edge of the input rating.** At the bottom of the input range the full 2 A charge draws about as much as the datasheet's recommended continuous current for the VIN pin. A 2 A adapter that sags toward 4.5 V will make the input voltage limit (VDPM) reduce the charge current, which is what that pin is for.

#### Battery temperature example

Take a thermistor and decide to stop charging below 0 °C and above 45 °C. The datasheet doesn't name a thermistor, so the resistances below use the beta model with values of our own: a 10 kΩ part with B = 3435 K and a 100 kΩ part with B = 4250 K. Check your thermistor's own table before using any of this.

|  | 10 kΩ thermistor (B = 3435 K) | 100 kΩ thermistor (B = 4250 K) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| RUT at 0 °C | 28.7 kΩ | 369 kΩ |
| ROT at 45 °C | 4.85 kΩ | 40.8 kΩ |
| First network: R1, R2 | 14.7 kΩ, 2.02 kΩ | 201 kΩ, 53.5 kΩ |
| Second network: R1, R2 | 9.67 kΩ, 68.9 kΩ | 76.1 kΩ, 281 kΩ |

With the nearest standard (E96) values for the first network and the 10 kΩ thermistor, R1 = 14.7 kΩ and R2 = 2 kΩ, the typical thresholds give trip points of −0.1 °C and 44.8 °C. The datasheet's tolerance bands on the thresholds (65.7–69.7% and 29.9–33.9%) spread the cold limit across −2.2 to 2.0 °C and the hot limit across 41.1 to 48.8 °C, before resistor and thermistor tolerances.

> **The divider drains the battery all the time.** It sits across the battery whether or not an adapter is present. At 3.7 V and 25 °C the 10 kΩ network draws about 139 µA, against the 20 µA maximum the datasheet gives for the part itself with boost off. The 100 kΩ network draws about 10 µA. That is one reason to prefer the larger thermistor in a power bank that sits on a shelf.

## Layout

Silergy gives these layout rules, along with a suggested placement drawing (Figure 4 in the datasheet).

- Maximize the **copper connected to the GND pin**, for both heat and noise.
- Place **CIN, L and CSYS** close to the device. Silergy's text lists CSYS twice; we'd guess the second is meant to be CBAT, but it doesn't say.
- Keep the loop formed by the **main switch, the rectifier switch and CSYS** as small as possible.
- Put **CIN as close as possible to the VIN and GND pins**.
- Keep the **copper on the LX pin small**, to reduce EMI.
- Place the small-signal resistors **RCHG and RSYS close to the device and away from the LX net**, to limit crosstalk.

The datasheet's thermal resistance (θJA) is 48 °C/W, measured on a JEDEC 51-3 four-layer test board (a low-conductivity one, per the datasheet's note), with 2.1 W of allowed power dissipation at 25 °C ambient. The package outline drawing includes a recommended pad layout, marked as reference only.

## What the datasheet shows

The typical-performance pages cover the circuit at 25 °C and 5 V in with RCHG = 2.55 kΩ and RSYS = 2.2 kΩ. They include efficiency against charge current, charge voltage and SYS current, scope captures of adapter insertion, charge soft start, constant-voltage and constant-current steady state, OTGB enabling and disabling boost, and boost steady state at 50 mA and 2 A. The remaining pages are the current-setting curves, which are the source of the points used above.

Reading the curves by eye: charge efficiency (the plot is titled constant-voltage mode, 5 V in) is about 95–96% from 0.3 A to 1 A, peaks near 96% around 0.8 A, and falls to about 92% at 2 A. In constant-current mode at 2 A it rises from about 91% at 2.7 V to about 91.7% at 4.1 V. For boost with a 3.7 V battery, efficiency is about 94% at 0.2 A, peaks near 96% around 0.8 A and falls to about 92% at 2.4 A. At 4.2 V it peaks near 97% around 0.6 A and is about 94% at 2.4 A, and at 3 V it peaks near 95% around 0.8 A and falls to about 87% at 2.4 A. Treat these as rough, about two points either way, since they come from graphs.

## Things to watch

- **C1 is rated 16 V, but the datasheet's text says above 16 V.** The input capacitor section asks for a voltage rating higher than 16 V, and the bill of materials fits a 16 V part. The pin tolerates surges to 18 V, so a 25 V part, the next common rating up, is a safer choice for C1 (our suggestion). The output capacitors are specified above 10 V, which the 16 V parts satisfy.
- **The input current limit in the reference circuit is above the pin's recommended current.** RIIN = 0.75 kΩ gives 2.5 A, but the recommended continuous current at VIN is 2 A, and the input overcurrent trip is 25% above the limit. A full 2 A charge needs about 1.8 A at 5 V in our estimate. For a smaller adapter, choose RIIN for that adapter.
- **The current-setting graphs run past the stated maximums.** The features say "maximum 2 A" for charge, but the RCHG graph runs up to about 2.5 A at 2 kΩ, and the RSYS graph to about 4 A at 1.5 kΩ, past the 2.5 A recommended continuous SYS current. Stay inside the 2 A and 2.5 A ratings.
- **The cell voltage accuracy is quoted two ways.** The features say ±0.5%, but the electrical table gives 4.324–4.376 V for a 4.35 V cell, which is about ±0.6%.
- **The charge-profile figure is only a sketch.** It shows a trickle threshold of 2.9 V and labels the trickle current "ICC/10, 50 mA". The electrical table gives a 2.6 V falling threshold with 200 mV of hysteresis, so about 2.8 V rising, and a trickle current of 10% of ICC. Use the tables.
- **Two junction-temperature ranges are listed.** The recommended operating conditions give both −20 to 100 °C and −40 to 85 °C, without saying which applies. The absolute maximum operating junction temperature is 125 °C.
- **Boost at full load from a low battery runs hot.** At 3 V and 2.4 A the efficiency curve reads about 87%, which means about 1.8 W of loss. With θJA = 48 °C/W that would be a rise of about 89 °C on the datasheet's test board if all of it were in the chip, which would put the junction above the 100 °C recommended maximum at 25 °C ambient. Part of the loss is in the inductor and the other parts, so the true figure is lower, but check the thermal margin on a real board. These are rough numbers, since they rest on an efficiency read by eye.
- **The snubber resistor is working hard.** Our estimate of C × V² × fSW is about 0.13 W, and 0805 resistors are commonly rated at 0.125 W. The datasheet doesn't say if the snubber is required, or give R10's power rating, so check the rating or use a smaller capacitor.
- **The reference circuit doesn't show what enables boost.** R8 holds OTGB high, which disables boost, so something has to pull the pin low. The pulse symbol beside the pin in the datasheet's schematic isn't explained; the OTGB scope capture shows boost running only while the pin is low.
- **The reference circuit has no thermistor.** With R9 not fitted, the OTGB pin sits above 90% of the battery voltage, so the temperature function is off and nothing stops charging a cold or hot cell. Add a network for any product that will be used in the cold or the heat.
- **R11 and R18 are in the bill of materials and unexplained.** R11 is a 100 kΩ resistor between the SYS rail and the battery node, beside unfitted parts (Q1, R14, R15) that look like an optional transistor path around it (our reading); R18 is a 0 Ω resistor at the right-hand edge of the schematic, near the output-port connections. Neither is explained, and the drawing here omits both. If you copy the datasheet's board, fit them as listed. If you don't, ask Silergy what they do first.
- **Many positions are unfitted.** The datasheet marks C2, C7, C9–C11, R9, R13–R17 and Q1–Q4 as NC and doesn't describe them. C2, C7, C9 and C10 sit in parallel with fitted capacitors, so they look like room for more capacitance (our reading).
- **Some faults latch.** A trickle timeout stops charging until the input is recycled, and a battery-depletion stop needs boost to be re-enabled. A product that should recover by itself has to do both.
- **It's a 5 V part.** The 18 V rating is for surviving a surge on VIN, not for charging from a higher voltage. Input overvoltage protection trips around 5.8 V, so don't feed it a 9 V or 12 V adapter.
