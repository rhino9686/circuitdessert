## How it works

The SY81153 is a synchronous buck regulator with both power switches inside a 1.6 × 1.6 mm package. It regulates with constant on-time, ripple-based control. The controller builds an internal copy of the inductor current, adds it to the feedback voltage, and starts a new on-time whenever that sum falls below the 0.6 V reference. The on-time is worked out inside the chip from the input voltage, the output voltage and the target frequency (tON = (VOUT ÷ VIN) ÷ fSW), so the switching frequency stays close to 500 kHz as VIN changes. The compensation is built in, so the circuit needs no external compensation network.

At light load the part switches to pulse-frequency modulation (PFM). Once the load falls below half the inductor ripple current, IOUT = VOUT × (1 − D) ÷ (2 × fSW × L), the bottom switch turns off before the current can reverse, and the controller skips cycles so the switching frequency falls with the load. In this circuit at 12 V in, with a 4.7 µH inductor, that crossover is about 0.51 A. Above it, the part runs in continuous conduction at nearly constant frequency. In PFM the part stops switching as soon as FB rises above the reference, so no separate over-voltage protection is needed.

Enabling the part (EN rising through about 1.2 V) starts a 180 µs turn-on delay, then an internal soft-start of roughly 1 ms that limits inrush current. The part can also start into an output that is already pre-biased. Current limiting is cycle by cycle: the top switch turns off at its peak limit (at least 4 A) and the bottom switch stays on until the current falls below its valley limit (at least 3 A). If the output drops below 33% of its regulated level, the part enters hiccup mode, 1.5 ms on and 4.5 ms off, and recovers by itself when the short is removed. Over-temperature protection shuts the part down at 150 °C and restarts it, after a full soft-start, once the junction has cooled by about 15 °C. Input under-voltage lockout keeps switching off until VIN is above its rising threshold.

The datasheet states a maximum duty cycle of up to 75% across temperature, so the output can't sit above roughly 75% of the input. For this 3.3 V circuit that works out to a VIN of about 4.4 V or more, just under the part's own 4.5 V minimum, so the limit doesn't bite at 3.3 V. It does at 5 V (see the table below).

The datasheet gives one reference circuit, for a 3.3 V output, and a table of component values for 1.2 V, 1.8 V and 5 V. It does not name a nominal input voltage, so the worked example below uses 12 V, with 18 V as the worst case for ripple and peak current.

### Output voltage

The output is set by two resistors: VOUT = 0.6 V × (1 + RH ÷ RL). The datasheet recommends 1 kΩ to 1 MΩ for both, to keep light-load current low. With RH = 100 kΩ and a 3.3 V target, RL = 0.6 ÷ (3.3 − 0.6) × 100 kΩ = 22.22 kΩ. The nearest 1% value, 22.1 kΩ, gives 3.315 V nominal. With the reference's ±1.5% tolerance the output lands between 3.265 V and 3.365 V, before the resistors' own 1% tolerance is added.

### Inductor

Choose the inductance for a ripple current of about 40% of the maximum load at the highest input voltage: L = VOUT × (1 − VOUT ÷ VIN,MAX) ÷ (fSW × IOUT,MAX × 0.4). For 3.3 V, 18 V in, 3 A and 500 kHz that gives 4.49 µH, and the datasheet uses 4.7 µH. The part tolerates a wide range of ripple, so the final value can differ from the calculation with little effect.

Two more checks apply. The saturation current must exceed the peak current, ISAT > IOUT,MAX + VOUT × (1 − VOUT ÷ VIN,MAX) ÷ (2 × fSW × L). And the winding resistance (DCR) should be below 50 mΩ for good efficiency, along with low core loss at 500 kHz.

### Output capacitor

Use an X5R or better ceramic rated for 16 V, with at least 22 µF. The reference circuit uses one 22 µF capacitor. Output ripple has two parts: the inductor ripple current across the capacitor's ESR (ΔIL × ESR), and the charge on the capacitance (ΔIL ÷ (8 × COUT × fSW)). Add them for a total. Ceramic capacitance falls as the DC voltage across it rises, so measured ripple is usually higher than the calculation; read the effective capacitance off the capacitor's DC-bias curve and recalculate.

### Input capacitor

Use an X5R or X7R ceramic rated 25 V (at least 20% above the highest input voltage), 10 µF or more, as close as possible to the IN and GND pins with the smallest possible loop. The RMS current it carries is IOUT × √(D × (1 − D)), at most IOUT ÷ 2 at 50% duty, so choose one with an RMS rating above half the load current, and add capacitors in parallel if the calculation needs it. The datasheet says capacitance matters less than RMS rating, and that a single 10 µF ceramic is enough for most applications. If VIN arrives over long wires, add bulk capacitance (electrolytic, tantalum or polymer) to damp the overshoot and ringing from the lead inductance. The reference circuit's 47 µF electrolytic, CIN1, is that kind of bulk capacitor; the datasheet doesn't say why it was included.

### Bootstrap, feedforward and enable

The bootstrap capacitor CBS is a 0.1 µF low-ESR ceramic between BS and LX; it supplies the gate drive for the top switch. The feedforward capacitor CFF sits across RH and can speed up the load-transient response; the datasheet recommends at least 22 pF for most applications and a larger value when the output LC is large. This circuit uses 22 pF.

Don't connect EN straight to VIN or another voltage source; the datasheet asks for a resistor between 1 kΩ and 1 MΩ, and EN must not float. The reference circuit uses RENH = 10 kΩ and RENL = 1 MΩ, which puts EN at 99% of VIN, so the part starts when VIN passes its own under-voltage threshold and the divider only limits current. To start at a higher voltage, size the divider from the EN thresholds (1.2 V rising, 1.0 V falling, typical): VIN,start = 1.2 V × (1 + RENH ÷ RENL). As an example, RENH = 562 kΩ with RENL = 100 kΩ starts the part at about 7.9 V and stops it at about 6.6 V.

> **The inductor margin is thin.** The datasheet lists VLS6045EX-4R7M as 3.8 A, only about 6% above the 3.57 A peak. Check on the inductor's own datasheet whether 3.8 A is its saturation rating or its heating rating, and at what temperature.

## Layout

Silergy gives these layout rules, along with a suggested placement drawing (Figure 9 in the datasheet). With a package this small and fast switching edges, layout decides how well the converter works.

- Put the **bootstrap capacitor** on the same layer as the IC and keep the BS, LX and CBS path as short as possible.
- Put the **input capacitors** very close to the IN and GND pins, with the smallest loop, joined by a wide copper area.
- Connect the **output capacitors'** negative terminals to the GND pin with wide copper, not vias.
- Place **RH, RL and CFF** as close to the FB pin as possible and route the feedback line away from LX and other fast signals. Take the feedback from the output capacitor with a Kelvin connection, not from the inductor's output terminal.
- Keep the **LX copper** small to limit EMI, but wide enough to keep its resistance and inductance low.
- Use a **ground plane** if you can. Put several vias on the ground pad, on a copper area larger than the pad, for heat. The datasheet's thermal resistance (θJA) is 100 °C/W on a 6 × 6 cm, two-layer, 2 oz board.

The pin table lists the exposed pad as "NC" (not connected), while the layout advice says to connect the exposed ground pad to copper with vias. Follow the layout advice, and confirm with Silergy if the pad matters for your design.

## What the datasheet shows

Silergy's typical-performance pages cover this circuit at 25 °C, 12 V in and 3.3 V out, with COUT = 22 µF and CFF = 10 pF (the reference circuit uses 22 pF). They include efficiency against load current from 1 mA to 3 A at 5 V, 12 V and 18 V in for each of the four output voltages, scope captures of output ripple at no load and full load, load-transient steps, start-up and shut-down from both EN and VIN, and a short-circuit capture that shows the hiccup behaviour.

Reading the 12 V in, 3.3 V out efficiency curve by eye, it is about 52% at 1 mA, about 87% at 0.1 A, about 92% around 1 A, and about 88% at 3 A. Treat these as rough, about two points either way, since they come from a graph. That plot uses 6.8 µH for 3.3 V, not the 4.7 µH of the reference circuit, so efficiency with a different inductor will differ a little.

## Things to watch

- **The CFF advice is not consistent across the datasheet.** The text recommends at least 22 pF, but its own table uses 10 pF for 1.2 V and 1.8 V, and the performance plots were taken with 10 pF. Start with the table value for your output voltage and adjust on the bench.
- **3 A in a 1.6 × 1.6 mm package needs copper.** The datasheet quotes 100 °C/W on its own test board; check junction temperature at your worst-case input voltage and ambient.
- **The inductor guidance mentions an FCCM light-load mode** that this part doesn't have. It looks carried over from a related datasheet; this part runs PFM.
- **There is no power-good pin.**
- **The listed output range goes up to 9.2 V, but the duty limit applies.** At 75% maximum duty the output can't sit above roughly three quarters of the input. The datasheet's own 5 V output curves start from 7.4 V in.

## Datasheet notes

The pin table lists the exposed pad as "NC" (not connected), but the layout section says to connect the exposed GND pad to a large copper area with several vias. Follow the layout advice and check the pad with Silergy. The inductor guidance also mentions an FCCM light-load mode, which this part doesn't list, probably carried over from a related datasheet.
