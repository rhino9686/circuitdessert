## How it works

The SY20757 is a bidirectional regulator for a one-cell power bank. A half bridge with integrated N-channel MOSFETs (18 mΩ on the high side and 9 mΩ on the low side, typical) connects the **BUS** node to the battery through the external inductor LB, so energy can flow either way. Charging runs it as a synchronous **buck** converter from the input at BUS down to the battery. Discharging runs it as a synchronous **boost** converter from the battery up to BUS, which feeds the two USB-A ports. Both directions use peak current mode control. The buck stage switches at about 400 kHz; the boost frequency is set by RF between 200 and 500 kHz. An internal state machine picks the mode, runs the charge phases, handles the ports and drives the fuel-gauge LEDs, so no microcontroller is needed.

There are four states. **Charging** runs when a source is present on Port B and both output ports are off. **Discharging** runs when the input is removed and at least one output port is on. **Bypass** runs when a source is present and at least one output port is on: the datasheet says the IC charges the battery and delivers power to the port from the adapter at the same time, and disables "the fast charging function". **Sleep** runs when the adapter and the load are both gone: Ports A1, A2 and B, VDD, the boost and the buck all turn off, and the battery drain is at most 50 µA.

**On the input side**, the IN pins connect through a reverse-blocking MOSFET (35 mΩ typical) to BUS, with overvoltage and under-voltage lockout on IN. Port B's data lines go to DPB and DMB, where the IC runs BC1.2 detection to find out what kind of source it has, and then sets the charge current, charge voltage, VDPM, IDPM and termination current "to minimize the charge time", in the datasheet's words. Two limiting loops protect the adapter. The **VDPM** loop reduces the buck duty cycle when the input voltage falls to 90% of the rated adapter voltage (4.5 V for a 5 V adapter, ±1.5%), so the adapter is not pulled below its rating. The **IDPM** loop does the same when the input current reaches the limit that the IC chose for the detected adapter (accurate to ±10% at its 2 A test point). The datasheet doesn't list the limits it chooses for each adapter type.

**Charging** follows the usual Li-Ion profile. Below 1.9–2.0 V the cell counts as shorted and the IC wakes it with a small charging current. Between that and the trickle threshold (2.7–2.9 V rising) it charges at 10% of ICHG, with a 2 hour timeout. Above that it charges at the constant current ICHG set by RCHG until the cell reaches the voltage chosen by the CV pin, then holds that voltage while the current falls. The charge ends at the termination current, which is 8% or 4% of ICHG depending on RITERM. A recharge starts if the cell falls by 100 mV. Charging stops if the cell voltage exceeds 103–107% of the CV setting (105% typical). Fast charge (constant current and constant voltage together) has a 20 hour timeout, and either timeout latches the charger off until the input is unplugged and plugged in again. The datasheet also says that in a normal cycle the IC "stops charging per 20s to measure the battery level", without saying for how long.

**Discharging** starts when a load is inserted on a port. The IC starts the boost converter and turns on that port's MOSFET, then identifies the device's charging protocol (the block diagram lists QC2.0/QC3.0, FCP, AFC, Apple 2.4 A mode and DCP) and changes the BUS voltage to the level the device asks for. When a second load appears, the IC supplies both ports at 5 V only, and the datasheet says it disables the fast-charge protocols when both ports are on. Each port is switched on its ground return in Figure 1 (our reading of the drawing; see Things to watch), and the current is measured across the sense resistor between SRPA and SRNA. A port turns off when its current exceeds 3.1–3.7 A (the overcurrent trip), or when it stays below 25–75 mA for 25 seconds (light-load shutdown). The total current is limited at 4 A typical (3.7–4.3 A) by regulating the boost duty cycle, and the BUS overvoltage trip is 108–112% of the regulated BUS voltage. The BUS voltage also rises with load current to make up for cable drop, as the section on termination current and cable compensation explains.

**Battery protection** in both directions includes thermal shutdown (150 °C, recovering at 130 °C) and a thermistor on the NTC pin. When charging, the thermistor sets three windows: no charging below 0 °C or above 45 °C, and half of ICHG between 0 °C and 15 °C. When discharging, the IC stops and goes to sleep below −20 °C or above 60 °C. Below the depletion threshold (VDEP, 3.0 V or 2.6 V, chosen by RLED1) the boost stops and the part sleeps; the datasheet gives 200 mV of hysteresis on the rising edge. The datasheet also lists protections against a short on BUS, and against the data lines shorting to BUS.

**The four LEDs** show the battery level and converter state. When charging, the LED for the current quarter flickers slowly and the ones below stay on; at 100% all four are on. When discharging, all lit LEDs flicker briefly and rarely, once per 2.56 seconds. A fault flickers all four slowly together. **The key** input on KEY/FSW does two things: one click resets the IC, and two clicks turn off light-load shutdown for 2 hours (useful for loads that draw under about 50 mA).

### Charge current (RCHG)

A resistor from the ICHG pin to ground sets the constant charge current: **ICHG = 12 × 10³ V ÷ RCHG**, with RCHG in ohms. The datasheet's test points are 2.4 kΩ for 5 A (±10%) and 6 kΩ for 2 A (±10%). The trickle current is 10% of ICHG and the termination current is 8% or 4% of it, so those scale with the same resistor. The worked-example section below tabulates the choices.

### Boost frequency (RF)

A resistor RF from BAT to KEY/FSW sets the boost frequency: **fSW = 18.5 × 10⁹ Ω·Hz ÷ RF**. The key switch pulls KEY/FSW to ground, so pressing the key puts RF across the battery, which draws VBAT ÷ RF while the key is held (about 99 µA at 3.7 V and 37.4 kΩ; our arithmetic). A lower frequency means lower switching loss but a larger inductor ripple; the datasheet gives no rule for choosing, only the range. The electrical table lists two boost frequencies for the same conditions (400/500/600 kHz and 200/250/300 kHz). The formula gives RF = 37 kΩ for 500 kHz and 74 kΩ for 250 kHz, which we take to be those two cases. The datasheet's 5 V boost scope capture shows about 10 cycles in 40 µs, which is about 250 kHz by our count, so the datasheet's plots may have been taken at the lower setting.

### Peak current and battery-resistance compensation (RPEAK1, RPEAK2)

Two resistors in series from the IPEAK pin to ground set the boost peak current limit: **IPEAK = 20 × 10³ V ÷ (RPEAK1 + RPEAK2)**. The RCOM pin connects to their midpoint. It sets the battery internal-resistance compensation, **RBAT_INTER = 45 × 10⁻⁶ × RPEAK2**. In boost mode the IC adds the compensation voltage (the discharge current times that resistance, by our reading) to the voltage on the BAT pin, to get the battery's real voltage for the depletion and fuel-gauge decisions. Because RPEAK2 is part of the total, the largest battery resistance you can compensate is 45 µ × (20 kV ÷ IPEAK) = 0.9 V ÷ IPEAK (our derivation). The datasheet gives no range for IPEAK, no tolerance on it, and no value for either resistor.

### Termination current and cable compensation (RITERM, RO_COMP)

The GA1/ITERM and GA2/VOCOM pins do two jobs. Each drives the gate of its port's MOSFET, and each has a resistor to ground that the IC reads when it wakes up. For GA1/ITERM, a resistor above 300 kΩ sets the termination current to 8% of ICHG and one below 50 kΩ sets 4%. For GA2/VOCOM, a resistor above 300 kΩ sets the BUS cable compensation to 0.25 V at 2 A, and one below 50 kΩ sets 0.375 V at 2 A. The compensation curve (Figure 4 in the datasheet) rises in a straight line from zero at no load to that value at 2 A, and stays flat above 2 A. With the 10 mΩ sense resistors, 0.25 V at 2 A works out to a slope of 125 mV per ampere, as though the cable were 125 mΩ, and 0.375 V to 187.5 mΩ (our arithmetic). The datasheet does not say what happens for resistors between 50 kΩ and 300 kΩ.

### Fuel-gauge LEDs (RLED1 to RLED4)

Each LED pin drives an LED to ground with a resistor in parallel. The resistors also program the thresholds. **RLED1** picks the depletion threshold: 100 kΩ (±20%) gives 3.0 V (2.95–3.05 V in the electrical table) and 50 kΩ (±20%) gives 2.6 V (2.6–2.7 V). **RLED2, RLED3 and RLED4** set the three level thresholds through their ratio to RLED1, using the datasheet's Table 3:

|  | Charging | Discharging |
| --- | --- | --- |
| VTH1 | VTH1 = 83% × VCV + 0.5 V × RLED2 ÷ RLED1 | VTH1 = 80% × VCV + 0.5 V × RLED2 ÷ RLED1 |
| VTH2 | VTH2 = 88% × VCV + 0.5 V × RLED3 ÷ RLED1 | VTH2 = 85% × VCV + 0.5 V × RLED3 ÷ RLED1 |
| VTH3 | VTH3 = 93% × VCV + 0.5 V × RLED4 ÷ RLED1 | VTH3 = 90% × VCV + 0.5 V × RLED4 ÷ RLED1 |

So each charging threshold sits 3% of VCV above its discharging partner, which is 125 mV at VCV = 4.178 V, and the same ratio sets both. The datasheet gives no LED type, no LED current and no suggested values for RLED2 to RLED4. The pins' absolute maximum is 3.3 V, so the LEDs' forward voltage has to be below that. The worked example gives values for some example thresholds.

### Port MOSFETs and sense resistors

The datasheet says the IC has two NMOS drivers for the ports' external MOSFETs, and its gate drive voltage is VDD (3.3 V), so each MOSFET needs a threshold well below that. In Figure 1 each port's USB ground goes through the sense resistor and then the MOSFET to ground, which means the MOSFET carries the full port current and its source node is the circuit ground. The MOSFET must also stand off whatever voltage the port's ground rises to when the port is off; with the load still connected that is up to the BUS voltage (our reading), so a drain rating comfortably above 12 V is needed for a 12 V BUS. The sense resistor is 10 mΩ in the datasheet's conditions. At the port overcurrent threshold of 3.1–3.7 A it drops 31–37 mV and dissipates up to 0.14 W at 3.7 A (our arithmetic), so use a resistor rated for at least 0.25 W. The datasheet doesn't discuss the resistor and diode drawn across each MOSFET; see Things to watch.

### Inductor

The datasheet bases the inductor on a ripple of about 40% of the average current, and says the part tolerates other amplitudes. For **buck** mode: LB = VBAT × (1 − VBAT ÷ VIN,MAX) ÷ (fSW × ICHG,MAX × 40%). For **boost** mode: LB = VBAT × (1 − VBAT ÷ VBUS,MAX) ÷ (fSW × IDIS,MAX × 40%). The datasheet defines IDIS,MAX only as the maximum discharge current. Its text says the ripple should be 40% of the average input current, which means the battery-side current, so we use that (the usual boost formula; see Things to watch). The saturation current must exceed the peak: ISAT,MIN > ICHG,MAX + VBAT × (1 − VBAT ÷ VIN,MAX) ÷ (2 × fSW × LB) in buck mode and the same with IDIS,MAX and VBUS,MAX in boost mode. The DCR and core loss at the switching frequency also affect efficiency. The datasheet doesn't name an inductor or give a current rating for its 2.2 µH test value. The worked example below puts numbers to these formulas; the highest peak current there is 9.7 A at 250 kHz (12 V out with a 2.7 V battery), so choose an inductor whose saturation current is above that, with margin.

### Capacitors

Use ceramic capacitors of X7R or better, because the datasheet recommends it for the input and battery capacitors. **CIN** on the IN pin is 10 µF, close to IN and GND, with the loop area kept small. **CBUS** should be at least 20 µF, close to BUS and GND. It is the buck stage's input capacitor and the boost stage's output capacitor, and the datasheet gives its ripple current as ICHG × √(D × (1 − D)) in buck mode and IBUS × √(D ÷ (1 − D)) in boost mode. **CBAT** should also be at least 20 µF. It is the buck stage's output capacitor and the boost stage's input capacitor, with ripple voltages VRIP,BAT,BUCK = (1 − D) × VBAT ÷ (8 × CBAT × fSW² × LB) and VRIP,BAT,BOOST = D × VBAT ÷ (8 × CBAT × fSW² × LB). Two small parts complete the set: **CBST** (0.1 µF from BST to LX) and **CVDD** (1 µF from VDD to ground). The datasheet gives no voltage ratings. CBUS sees the regulated BUS voltage and, in a fault, up to the overvoltage trip at 108–112% of it (up to 13.4 V at 12 V), so a rating of 25 V or more is sensible for CIN and CBUS (our suggestion), and ceramic capacitance falls as the DC voltage across it rises, so check each part's bias curve against the 20 µF minimums.

### Battery temperature network

The thermistor circuit is fixed: RNTC_UP (10 kΩ) from VDD to the NTC pin and a 10 kΩ 103-AT thermistor from the pin to ground. The comparator thresholds are percentages of VDD (3.3 V) and are given for that thermistor, so with other parts the temperatures move. The worked example below checks the stated thresholds against a thermistor model. The divider is fed from VDD, which is off in sleep mode, so it should not count against the 50 µA sleep figure (our reading of the block diagram); with VDD on it draws about 165 µA at 25 °C (3.3 V across 20 kΩ).

> **A 5 V adapter can't deliver 5 A of charge current.** The full 5 A into a 4.328 V cell needs about 4.8 A at 5 V. The IC sets its input current limit from the adapter it detects, so an adapter rated below that makes the IDPM loop cut the charge current. A 9 V adapter would need at least 2.4 A and a 12 V adapter at least 1.8 A for the same 5 A (lossless lower bounds, our arithmetic), but the datasheet gives no charging efficiency above 5 V in.

> **The loss at full current is large for the package.** At 5 A the loss is about 2.1 W. With θJA = 43 °C/W, that is a temperature rise of up to 92 °C if all of it were in the chip (an upper bound, since part of it is in the inductor and the sense path).

#### Buck inductor

#### Input voltage limit

VDPM is 90% of the rated adapter voltage, which the datasheet states for a 5 V adapter (4.5 V, ±1.5%, so 4.43–4.57 V). Applying the same 90% to the other common adapters (our arithmetic) gives 8.1 V for 9 V and 10.8 V for 12 V. The datasheet gives the input overvoltage threshold only for a 5 V adapter (5.7–6.0 V rising, 0.1 V hysteresis).

> **VDPM sits close to the under-voltage lockout on a 5 V adapter.** The rising lockout threshold is 4.2–4.55 V with 100 mV of hysteresis, so by our subtraction the falling threshold is 4.10–4.45 V. The lowest VDPM level (4.43 V) is below the highest falling threshold (4.45 V), so on a unit with both at those extremes a sagging adapter could drop the input out before VDPM holds it. The overlap only occurs at the extremes of both tolerance bands.

#### Battery temperature check

The thresholds are percentages of VDD, so the thermistor resistance at each trip point follows from the 10 kΩ pull-up: R = 10 kΩ × K ÷ (1 − K). That value is our assumption for a 103-AT part, since the datasheet doesn't give one, and the last column is the temperature spread across the datasheet's minimum-to-maximum band.

## Layout

Silergy gives these layout rules, along with a recommended placement drawing (Figure 9 in the datasheet).

- Place **LB, CIN and CBUS close to the IC**, for the best efficiency and the least noise.
- Maximize the **copper connected to the GND pins**, for both heat and noise. If the board has room, use a ground plane.
- Place **CBUS close to the BUS and GND pins**, and keep the loop that CBUS and GND form as small as possible.
- Place **CIN close to the IN and GND pins**, with the same small loop.
- Keep the **copper area on the LX pin as small as possible**, to avoid noise problems.
- In high-current designs, add an **RC snubber between LX and GND** for better EMI. The datasheet gives no values.

The datasheet's thermal resistance (θJA) is 43 °C/W on a four-layer JEDEC 51-7 board, θJC is 16 °C/W, and the power dissipation limit is 3.6 W at 25 °C. The package outline drawing includes a recommended pad layout, marked as reference only. The recommended junction temperature range is −20 to 120 °C.

## What the datasheet shows

The typical-performance pages are for 25 °C and 5 V in. They include efficiency against output current in boost mode at 5, 9 and 12 V (each for batteries at 2.7 V, 2.9 V and 3.7 V), and efficiency against charging current in buck mode, at VBAT = 4.328 V. The rest are scope captures: steady state in buck mode and in boost mode at 5, 9 and 12 V; buck power-on and power-off; Port A1 turning on at a load insertion and off at a key press; boost transitions from 5 V to 9 V and from 9 V to 12 V; charge soft start; a battery short in charging mode; a Port A1 short circuit; and the NTC protection in charging and discharging.

Reading the curves by eye: in boost mode the efficiency peaks at 96–97% for a 3.7 V battery at 5 V out near 1 A, and falls to about 94% at 3.6 A. At 9 V it peaks near 94.6% at about 1 A and falls to about 93% at 2 A, and at 12 V it peaks near 93% at about 0.9 A and falls to about 92% at 1.5 A. For a 2.7 V battery the curves peak about 1 point lower and fall faster: about 88% at 5 V and 3.6 A, 87% at 9 V and 2 A, and 86% at 12 V and 1.5 A. In buck mode the efficiency peaks near 96.6% at about 1.5 A and falls to about 93% at 4 A and 91% at 5 A. Treat these as rough, about two points either way, since they come from graphs.

The NTC captures are consistent with the thresholds above. With VDD at 3.3 V, the charging captures run VNTC between 2.6 V, 2.2 V and 1.5 V (79%, 67% and 45% of VDD), which are the cold, cool and normal bands. The discharging captures use 1.5 V, 2.9 V (88%) and 0.5 V (15%), which are the normal, cold and hot bands (our arithmetic against the table).

## Things to watch

- **The datasheet has no bill of materials, and few values.** It gives CIN, LB, CBUS, CBAT, CBST, CVDD, RS1, RS2, RNTC_UP, RNTC and RLED1, and names no part. RCHG, RF, RPEAK1, RPEAK2, RITERM, RO_COMP, RLED2 to RLED4, the MOSFETs, the inductor and the LEDs are yours to choose, and the values on this page for them are calculated or examples, not Silergy's.
- **The port MOSFETs are unspecified.** The IC drives them at 3.3 V (VDD), so they need a logic-level gate threshold. In Figure 1 they carry the port current on the ground return (our reading of the drawing), where the voltage they must block depends on how the port ground floats, and the datasheet names no rating. It also draws a resistor and a diode across each MOSFET and says nothing about them. We can't say whether they set a bias, form a path for reverse current, or represent the MOSFET's own body diode; ask Silergy before leaving them out.
- **The electrical table gives two boost frequencies for one set of conditions.** The rows read 400/500/600 kHz and 200/250/300 kHz for VBAT = 3.7 V and VBUS = 5 V, with no RF. The formula gives about 37 kΩ and 74 kΩ for the typical values. The datasheet's efficiency plots and scope captures don't state their frequency either; by our count the boost capture is near 250 kHz and the buck capture is near 400 kHz.
- **The boost inductor equation's current is ambiguous.** The datasheet writes the boost formula with IDIS,MAX ("the maximum discharge current") but says the ripple should be 40% of the average input current. Read as the battery-side current it is the standard boost formula, which is what this page uses; read as the BUS-side current it would give an inductance larger than the 40% target needs, by roughly the ratio VBUS ÷ VBAT.
- **IPEAK has no stated range or tolerance, and the output current limit alone doesn't hold the power at 18 W.** The total current limit (3.7–4.3 A) is about 18.5–21.5 W at 5 V but about 48 W at 12 V. The datasheet doesn't say what holds 9 V and 12 V outputs to 18 W; our reading is the IPEAK setting, but it doesn't link the two. It also gives no switch current rating, and our calculation shows peaks of 8.7 to 9.7 A at a 2.7 V battery.
- **Full power from a nearly empty cell dissipates a lot in a 5 × 5 mm package.** The worst loss in the table is 2.9 W (for 12 V out with a 2.7 V battery). At θJA = 43 °C/W that would raise the junction by up to 126 °C if all of it were in the chip, which would exceed the 120 °C recommended maximum at 25 °C ambient. Part of the loss is in the inductor, the sense resistors and the board, so the true figure is lower, and at 5 V out with a 3.7 V battery the same estimate is 49 °C. Check the thermal margin on a real board, and consider setting the depletion threshold to 3.0 V (RLED1 = 100 kΩ).
- **The datasheet is only half specified above 5 V in.** The overvoltage trip, the charge-efficiency plot and the input current limits are given for a 5 V adapter only. The input range (4.6–13.2 V) and the 90% VDPM rule imply that 9 V and 12 V adapters are accepted, but the datasheet doesn't say how the IC asks for the higher voltage, or what it does with an adapter that doesn't.
- **The CV settings are 4.178 V, 4.328 V and 4.378 V.** Floating gives a charge voltage just under 4.2 V. The other two are for cells rated above 4.2 V, which the datasheet doesn't name. Choose the setting from your cell's data sheet; a wrong setting overcharges the cell.
- **The termination and compensation resistors have gaps.** The datasheet defines only two ranges for each (above 300 kΩ and below 50 kΩ), so values between them are undefined. It reads them at wake-up from the same pins that drive the MOSFET gates. Its electrical table gives the termination current tolerance for "RICHG = 2.4 kΩ", which we read as RCHG.
- **"Fast charging" in bypass mode is ambiguous.** The datasheet says the IC "disables the fast charging function" in bypass mode and doesn't say whether it means the battery's constant-current phase or the fast-charge protocols on the ports. The fast-charge section uses "fast charging protocols" for the QC, FCP and AFC handshakes and the electrical table uses "fast charge" for the constant-current and constant-voltage phases, and the bypass description doesn't say which it means.
- **The LED pins are only rated for 3.3 V, and no LED current is given.** Figure 7 shows each LED with a resistor in parallel and no series resistor, and the datasheet gives no LED type or current. Test the brightness and the forward voltage of your LED against the 3.3 V pin limit before choosing one.
- **VDPM is close to the under-voltage lockout on a 5 V adapter.** On a unit at the extremes of both tolerance bands, the VDPM level of 4.43 V is below the falling lockout of 4.45 V (our arithmetic; see the worked example). A weak adapter could drop out instead of being held at the VDPM level.
- **Several labels in the datasheet don't match.** The pin table describes SRPA2 as the current-sense positive pin of Port A1 (it should say A2). The 20 hour fast-charge timeout sits in the Min column and the 2 hour trickle timeout in the Typ column. The text says depletion is 3.0 V or 2.6 V, and the table says 2.95–3.05 V and 2.6–2.7 V. The recommended junction range starts at −20 °C, while the ambient range runs down to −40 °C.
- **Some behaviours latch or pause.** A trickle or fast-charge timeout latches the charger off until the input is replugged. Light-load shutdown turns a port off after 25 seconds below 25–75 mA, which can catch a device that has nearly finished charging (two key clicks turn it off for 2 hours). And the IC stops charging periodically to measure the battery, which might show as dips in the charge current.
- **Mind the headroom.** The input range is 4.6–13.2 V, but the absolute maximum on the power pins is 18 V and the recommended range stops at 16 V. A 12 V quick-charge adapter with spikes needs care.
- **The fuel-gauge patterns mean something.** While charging, the LED for the current band blinks slowly. While discharging, all the active LEDs blink briefly and rarely, and all four blink together on a fault.
