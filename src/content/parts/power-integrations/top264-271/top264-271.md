## How it works

The TOP264 to TOP271 are off-line flyback switch ICs. Each puts a 725 V power MOSFET, a high-voltage start-up source, the oscillator, the error amplifier and the protection circuits in one package, so a complete supply needs only the transformer, a clamp, the output stage and an optocoupler. The eight devices differ in MOSFET size: the typical current limit runs from 1.30 A for the TOP264 to 5.17 A for the TOP271, and RDS(ON) falls from 6.25 Ω to 1.2 Ω maximum at 25 °C (see the *current limit and on-resistance* table). The package is chosen by the letter after the device number: E is eSIP-7C, V is eDIP-12 and K is eSOP-12.

Start-up and supply come through the **CONTROL (C)** pin. When rectified high voltage is applied to DRAIN, an internal current source charges the capacitor on C. At about 5.8 V the control circuit starts and a 17 ms soft-start ramps the peak drain current and the switching frequency up from low starting values. If feedback current reaches C before the capacitor has fallen to the 4.8 V lower threshold, the shunt regulator on C holds 5.8 V and the current above what the chip consumes sets the duty cycle. The dynamic impedance of the pin, ZC (13, 21 or 25 Ω depending on the device), sets the error-amplifier gain. In a fault, such as an open loop or a shorted output, C falls to 4.8 V and the part enters auto-restart, switching at about 2% duty cycle and 0.5 Hz until the fault clears.

The **FREQUENCY (F)** pin sets the full-load switching frequency: tied to SOURCE it gives 132 kHz, and tied to CONTROL it gives 66 kHz. The datasheet advises against leaving it open. Jitter of ±5 kHz (±2.5 kHz at 66 kHz) at 250 Hz spreads the EMI.

### Multi-mode control

The modulator changes how it regulates as the load falls, which is what keeps light-load and no-load losses low. The thresholds are fractions of the current limit that is set (by the device, or lower through the X pin):

| Mode | Where it applies | What changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full-frequency PWM | Peak current stays above 55% of the set limit | Duty cycle falls by shortening the on-time at 132 or 66 kHz, as in earlier TOPSwitch parts |
| Variable-frequency PWM | Peak current has fallen to 55% of the limit | Peak current is held at 55% and the frequency drops from full frequency toward 30 kHz; jitter fades out |
| Low-frequency PWM | Frequency has reached 30 kHz | Frequency is held at 30 kHz and the on-time shortens, taking peak current from 55% toward 25% of the limit |
| Multi-cycle modulation | Peak current has fallen to 25% of the limit | Bursts of 4 or 5 pulses at 30 kHz, each at 25% of the limit, then off until the CONTROL current falls below IC(OFF) |

For a TOP269 at its typical 3.48 A limit, 55% and 25% are about 1.9 A and 0.87 A. For a TOP266 at 2.55 A they are about 1.4 A and 0.64 A (calculated here).

### The V and X pins

The **VOLTAGE MONITOR (V)** pin takes current from the DC bus through a resistor RLS. Its thresholds are set in current, so the line voltage at which they trip is the threshold current times RLS plus the pin voltage of about 3 V:

- Undervoltage: IUV is 25 µA typical (22–27 µA), so the supply starts only above VUV = IUV × RLS + VV. The datasheet gives 14 µA of hysteresis.
- Overvoltage: IOV is 112 µA typical (107–117 µA), with 4 µA of hysteresis. Above it the part stops switching until the line comes back.
- Line feed-forward: from about 22 µA of V-pin current the maximum duty cycle is cut, at −1.0 % per µA up to 48 µA and −0.25 % per µA above that, down to a 30% minimum at 95 µA. This lowers the duty cycle at high line and helps with ripple rejection, so a smaller input capacitor can be used.
- Latching shutdown: a V-pin current above 336 µA (269–403 µA) for 100 µs latches the part off. A latch is cleared by pulling X-pin current of −27 µA or more negative, or by letting VC fall below its reset voltage. The two example designs use this for output over-voltage, by pushing current into V from the bias winding.

The **EXTERNAL CURRENT LIMIT (X)** pin sets the current limit by a resistor to SOURCE, between about 30% and 100% of the device's limit. That lets a larger device run at a lower limit, which is the trick Figure 25 uses. A resistor from the bus to X lowers the limit as the line voltage rises, to give a roughly flat overload power. The X pin also works as a remote on/off input and as a fast AC reset for the latch.

The datasheet says what it was trying to do:

- **One device size larger than the power table asks for.** The table's 85–265 Vac adapter column with a metal heat sink gives 70 W for a TOP268EG; the design uses the TOP269EG (80 W in that column). The X pin sets the current limit down, so overload power, the transformer and the other parts are sized as for the smaller device, but the larger MOSFET has lower conduction loss. The datasheet notes that going larger again often gives the same or lower efficiency because of the larger switching losses.
- **Zener bleed clamp instead of an RCD clamp.** In an RCD clamp the capacitor is discharged by a parallel resistor, and at light load the capacitor falls well below the reflected voltage, so the energy to recharge it each cycle is wasted. With the Zener in series the clamp capacitor can't discharge below the Zener voltage, and the clamp dissipation at light and no load goes down. D2 is an 800 V diode with a slow 500 ns recovery, chosen so a little clamp energy returns during recovery.
- **High reflected voltage.** The datasheet gives VOR = 110 V, which lets the output diode be a 100 V Schottky with a low forward voltage (0.455 V at 5 A).
- **Low feedback current.** U2 is a 1.24 V LMV431 rather than a 2.5 V TL431, which cuts its supply current from 1 mA to 100 µA. Q2 forms a Darlington with the optocoupler transistor U3B, which brings the secondary feedback current down to about 1 mA, and R16 is raised and R25 added to compensate for the extra gain.
- **Bias winding at about 9 V at no-load.** The number of bias turns and C10 are chosen so the voltage across C10 stays near 9 V at no load, the least that keeps the optocoupler biased.
- **Line-sense resistors from 4 MΩ to 10.2 MΩ, with R20.** This saves about 16 mW at no-load. See the worked checks below.
- **Q1 and VR1 for over-voltage shutdown.** In an open-loop condition the output and bias voltages rise; when the bias rail passes VR1 plus a base-emitter drop, Q1 turns on and feeds the V pin. Q1 makes sure the current is enough to pass the 336 µA latch threshold even at full load and low line, where the output overshoot is small.
- **R7, R8 and R9 limit overload power as the line rises.** This keeps the overload power roughly flat, below the 100 VA limit for a limited power source. To cover a single fault such as R8 opening, the bias rise during an overload also triggers the latching shutdown.

The design choices the datasheet lists for this one:

- **One size smaller than the table.** The table's size for the 85–265 Vac open-frame, PCB-copper column is TOP267V (32 W). The design uses the TOP266V (28.5 W in that column) because the ambient is 40 °C rather than the table's 50 °C and the PCB area was laid out for it. The measured worst case was 107 °C at the device at full load, 40 °C, 85 Vac and 47 Hz.
- **132 kHz allows a smaller core**, with little efficiency cost because the TOPSwitch-JX has a small drain capacitance compared with discrete MOSFETs.
- **A 60 V Schottky.** The high reflected voltage lets the output diode be a 60 V part instead of 80 V or 100 V, and the lower forward drop raises efficiency. Two 5 A, 60 V axial diodes in parallel (the datasheet recommends each be rated at twice the output current, on a shared cathode copper area so their temperatures track) allow PCB heat-sinking.
- **L2 as a soft-finish.** To limit output overshoot at start-up, the voltage across L2 drives the optocoupler LED through D10 and U2A once it exceeds their forward drop. That limits the rate of rise of the output and replaces the capacitor that is usually placed across the shunt reference.
- **The same low-current feedback ideas**: a 300–600% CTR optocoupler brings the LED current from about 3 mA to under 1 mA, the LMV431 takes 100 µA instead of 1 mA, and C7 and the bias turns hold about 9 V at no-load.
- **Line-sense resistors 4 MΩ to 10.2 MΩ with R12**, as in Figure 25.

## Layout

The datasheet gives these rules for V, K and E packages, with a layout drawing for each (Figures 29 to 31):

- Connect the **SOURCE pin and the bias-winding return at the negative of the input capacitor with a Kelvin connection**, so the high switching current does not flow through the bias or control returns. The bias-winding return goes to the bulk capacitor on a dedicated trace, not to SOURCE.
- Put the **CONTROL pin bypass capacitor close to the C and S pins**. A 47 µF capacitor with a parallel high-frequency capacitor also goes on the CONTROL supply.
- Keep the **optocoupler output near the C and S pins**, away from the drain and clamp nodes.
- Keep the **clamp loop small**: the drain, clamp diode, clamp capacitor and transformer.
- Keep the **V-pin resistors within 10 mm of the V pin**, with no capacitance on V and no switching traces next to it.
- Put the **Y capacitor near the secondary return and the primary DC+**, or on a dedicated trace if it returns to the bulk capacitor's negative.
- The **exposed pad is tied to SOURCE** and must not be tied to other primary ground nodes. Heat-sink copper goes under it.
- After building, run the datasheet's benchmark tests: peak VDS at or under 675 V at high line and maximum overload, drain current at start-up and the leading-edge spike, and thermal margin to allow for the spread in RDS(ON).

## Things to watch

- **The datasheet's power table assumes conditions your design may not meet.** 12 V output, a Schottky output diode, 135 V reflected voltage, a 100 Vdc (85–265 Vac) or 250 Vdc (230 Vac) minimum bus, 50 °C ambient and a device temperature of 110 °C or less. Figure 26's design departs from it, as the datasheet explains (a 40 °C ambient and a layout made for the part).
- **R20 widens the undervoltage tolerance and effectively turns off the line overvoltage.** With 10.2 MΩ and a 191 kΩ resistor to CONTROL the undervoltage point is near 95 Vdc but the spread of IUV and of the R20 current moves it by tens of volts, and the overvoltage point goes from about 450 Vdc to about 980 Vdc. The datasheet says as much; in both designs the output over-voltage protection is the latch from the bias winding, not the V pin's line sense.
- **The latch depends on the bias winding.** The over-voltage latch in both designs is fed from the bias rail, so the bias voltage must follow the output reliably, and the datasheet adds Q1 and VR1 in Figure 25 so the latch current is enough at low line and full load.
- **No-load input power depends on tuning the bias winding.** Both designs set the minimum bias voltage at about 9 V at no load. The datasheet sets the bias turns and the bias capacitor (C10 or C7) for this, so changing either changes the no-load margin.
- **The efficiency tables don't say how they were measured.** The datasheet gives no ambient temperature or averaging method, and the average-efficiency column is blank at the lowest line voltage in both tables.
- **The V package's footprint is easy to get mirrored.** Its pins run clockwise seen from above, the opposite of an ordinary DIP. A footprint numbered like an ordinary DIP swaps the SOURCE pins with V, X, C, F and DRAIN. A SamacSys TOP264VG library we compared is numbered that way, and also has a plated hole at the missing pin 5, 0.6 mm from the DRAIN pad; the package page has the details.
- **The KiCad files are ours and have not been opened in KiCad.** The three footprints and the symbols were generated by script and checked by an independent parse against the datasheet's drawings and pin table. Use the symbol that matches the package: the eSIP-7C numbers SOURCE and DRAIN differently, and the eSOP-12B is the mirror image of the eDIP-12B although the pin numbers are the same. The eSIP-7C's hole and pad sizes are ours, because the datasheet gives only the positions.
- **Package names disagree inside the datasheet.** Table 1 and the ordering information say eDIP-12 and eSOP-12, while the package drawings and the revision history say the eDIP-12B and eSOP-12B packages replaced them in Rev. E. This page uses eDIP-12B and eSOP-12B.
